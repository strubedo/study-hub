## Why media still matters

Most real outages start at Layer 1: the wrong patch cable, a dirty fiber, an optic that does not match the far end, or a cable run that is quietly 130 m long. The v2.0 blueprint uses the verb **diagnose** for topic 1.1, so you need to know the media rules well enough to look at a symptom and name the cause.

## UTP categories

Unshielded twisted pair (UTP) has four twisted pairs terminated in an 8-pin modular connector (commonly called RJ-45). The category defines how much bandwidth (in MHz) the cable is certified for, which in turn limits the Ethernet speed it can carry.

| Category | Rated bandwidth | Ethernet it is normally used for | Max channel length |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cat5 | 100 MHz | 100BASE-TX (legacy; no longer a recognized TIA category) | 100 m |
| Cat5e | 100 MHz | 1000BASE-T, 2.5GBASE-T | 100 m |
| Cat6 | 250 MHz | 1000BASE-T, 5GBASE-T; 10GBASE-T only to about 55 m | 100 m (55 m at 10G) |
| Cat6a | 500 MHz | 10GBASE-T | 100 m |
| Cat8 | 2000 MHz | 25GBASE-T / 40GBASE-T (data center) | 30 m |

The **100 m** copper limit is a *channel* limit: up to 90 m of permanent horizontal cable plus up to 10 m of patch cords in total. Beyond that, signal attenuation and crosstalk grow, and you start to see CRC errors, link flaps, or a link that negotiates down to a lower speed.

> **Exam tip:** If a question says a copper run is 120 m and the port shows CRC errors or the link flaps, the length is the answer. The fix is a shorter run, an intermediate switch, or fiber.

> **Real world:** 2.5GBASE-T and 5GBASE-T (IEEE 802.3bz, Cisco "mGig") exist so Wi-Fi 6/6E access points can exceed 1 Gbps over the Cat5e/Cat6 already in the walls. Check the switch port type before blaming the cable when an AP links at 1 Gbps.

## Pinouts: straight-through, crossover, rollover

10BASE-T and 100BASE-TX use only two pairs: pins **1-2** and **3-6**.

* **MDI** devices (PCs, routers, servers, firewalls, access points) transmit on 1-2 and receive on 3-6.
* **MDI-X** devices (switches and hubs) do the opposite: transmit on 3-6, receive on 1-2.

So a cable must connect one side's transmit pair to the other side's receive pair.

| Cable | Wiring | Classic use |
|---|---|---|
| Straight-through | Same standard both ends (T568B-T568B or T568A-T568A) | Unlike devices: PC-switch, router-switch, AP-switch |
| Crossover | T568A one end, T568B the other (1-2 swapped with 3-6) | Like devices: switch-switch, router-router, PC-PC, PC-router |
| Rollover (console) | Pin 1 to 8, 2 to 7 ... 8 to 1 | PC serial adapter to an RJ-45 console port |

Note that a **router and a PC are both MDI**, so a direct PC-to-router Ethernet connection classically needs a crossover cable. A full Gigabit crossover also swaps pairs 4-5 and 7-8, but you will rarely need one because of the next feature.

### Auto-MDIX

Auto-MDIX lets a port detect the cable type and internally swap its transmit and receive pairs, so either cable works. On Catalyst switches it is enabled by default (`mdix auto`), but it depends on speed auto-negotiation: on many platforms, if you hard-code the speed (and on some older platforms, the duplex) the port stops performing Auto-MDIX and the wrong cable type results in a dead link.

```ios
SW1(config)# interface GigabitEthernet1/0/24
SW1(config-if)# speed auto
SW1(config-if)# duplex auto
SW1(config-if)# mdix auto
```

1000BASE-T uses all four pairs bidirectionally and requires auto-negotiation, and Gigabit PHYs correct pair swaps on their own.

> **Watch out:** "The link is down after we hard-coded speed 100 and duplex full, and the switch-to-switch run uses a straight-through cable" is a classic Auto-MDIX trap. Either set speed back to auto or use a crossover cable.

## Fiber: single-mode vs multimode

Fiber carries light in a glass core surrounded by cladding. It is immune to EMI, does not create ground loops between buildings, and runs much farther than copper.

| Property | Multimode (MMF) | Single-mode (SMF) |
|---|---|---|
| Core diameter | 50 or 62.5 µm | about 9 µm (8-10) |
| Light source | LED / VCSEL, mostly 850 nm | Laser, 1310 nm or 1550 nm |
| Distance | Hundreds of meters | Kilometers (10, 40, 80 km and beyond) |
| Limiting factor | Modal dispersion (many light paths) | Attenuation / chromatic dispersion |
| Optic cost | Lower | Higher |
| Typical jacket color | OM1/OM2 orange, OM3/OM4 aqua, OM5 lime | Yellow |

The OM grade matters for 10G: 10GBASE-SR reaches roughly 300 m on OM3 and 400 m on OM4, but only about 26-33 m on old 62.5 µm OM1.

### Connectors and polarity

| Connector | Description |
|---|---|
| **LC** | Small form-factor, push-latch, 1.25 mm ferrule. Standard on SFP/SFP+ optics. |
| **SC** | Larger square push-pull connector, 2.5 mm ferrule. Common on older gear and patch panels. |
| **ST** | Round bayonet twist-lock. Legacy multimode. |
| **MPO/MTP** | Multi-fiber (12/16/24) array for parallel optics such as 40GBASE-SR4 and 100GBASE-SR4. |

A duplex link uses two fibers, and each optic's **Tx must reach the far end's Rx**. If the strands are crossed wrong (Tx to Tx), the link stays down and both sides report very low or no receive power. BiDi optics use one strand with two different wavelengths.

> **Real world:** Fiber end faces are polished UPC (usually blue connector body) or APC (green, 8-degree angle). Mating UPC to APC causes large loss. Always clean and inspect a connector before declaring an optic dead.

## Transceivers: SFP and friends

Modular, hot-swappable transceivers let one switch port take copper or many types of fiber.

| Form factor | Speed | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| SFP | 1 Gbps (some 100 Mbps) | 1000BASE-SX/LX/T, LC duplex |
| SFP+ | 10 Gbps | Same size as SFP; most SFP+ ports also accept a 1G SFP |
| SFP28 | 25 Gbps | Often accepts SFP+ at 10G |
| QSFP+ / QSFP28 | 40 / 100 Gbps | Four lanes; MPO or LC (for LR4) |

Rules that show up in troubleshooting questions:

* Both ends must use **compatible optics**: same wavelength and fiber type (SX to SX over MMF, LR to LR over SMF). An SR optic on one end and an LR optic on the other will not link.
* An SFP port cannot run 10G; putting an SFP+ into a 1G-only SFP slot typically fails or is rejected.
* A 10G SFP+ port with a 1G SFP runs at 1G, and the far end must also be 1G.
* Direct-attach copper (DAC/Twinax) cables are SFP+ ends with a fixed cable, used for short (a few meters) runs inside a rack.

## Decoding the standard names

The name is *speed* + *BASE* (baseband) + *media code*. T = twisted pair, S = short wavelength (850 nm), L = long wavelength (1310 nm), E = extended (1550 nm).

| Standard | Media | Wavelength | Typical reach |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1000BASE-T | Cat5e or better, 4 pairs | n/a | 100 m |
| 1000BASE-SX | MMF | 850 nm | 220 m (62.5 µm) to 550 m (50 µm) |
| 1000BASE-LX | SMF (also MMF to 550 m with a mode-conditioning cord) | 1310 nm | 5 km per IEEE; Cisco LX/LH optics are rated 10 km |
| 10GBASE-T | Cat6a (Cat6 to 55 m) | n/a | 100 m |
| 10GBASE-SR | MMF | 850 nm | 300 m OM3 / 400 m OM4 |
| 10GBASE-LR | SMF | 1310 nm | 10 km |
| 10GBASE-ER | SMF | 1550 nm | 40 km |

> **Exam tip:** Read the letter, not the number. "S" means multimode and short; "L" and "E" mean single-mode and long. If the question says two buildings 3 km apart, the answer is single-mode with LX/LR optics, not SX/SR.

## Picking media: a quick decision guide

1. **Within a rack, under 100 m, normal office**: UTP (Cat6/6a for new builds) or DAC between switches.
2. **Between closets in one building, more than 100 m, or 10G over existing MMF**: multimode with SX/SR.
3. **Between buildings or kilometers away**: single-mode with LX/LR/ER.
4. **Electrically noisy areas (factory floor, near motors) or between buildings with different grounding**: fiber, regardless of distance.

## Key takeaways

* Copper Ethernet is limited to **100 m** (90 m permanent link + 10 m patch). Cat5e handles 1 Gbps, Cat6a handles 10 Gbps at 100 m, Cat6 only to about 55 m at 10 Gbps.
* Like devices need a crossover, unlike devices a straight-through, unless **Auto-MDIX** is working, and Auto-MDIX generally needs speed auto-negotiation.
* PCs and routers are MDI; switches are MDI-X. PC-to-router is a "like" connection.
* Multimode = large core, 850 nm, hundreds of meters; single-mode = 9 µm core, 1310/1550 nm lasers, kilometers.
* LC is the small connector used on SFP/SFP+; SC is the larger square push-pull type.
* Both ends of a fiber link need matching optics and correct Tx-to-Rx polarity.
* SX/SR = multimode short reach; LX/LR = single-mode long reach; T = copper twisted pair.
