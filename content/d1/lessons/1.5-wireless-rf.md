## Wi-Fi is shared, half-duplex radio

All stations on a channel share the same air. Only one can transmit at a time, so 802.11 uses **CSMA/CA** (listen before talk, random backoff, acknowledgments) instead of Ethernet's CSMA/CD. Almost every wireless performance problem comes back to three things: **how much spectrum you have** (band and channel), **how strong the signal is relative to the noise**, and **who else is transmitting** on or near your channel.

## 802.11 standards at a glance

| Standard | Wi-Fi name | Bands | Max channel width | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 802.11b | (none) | 2.4 GHz | 22 MHz | 11 Mbps, DSSS |
| 802.11a | (none) | 5 GHz | 20 MHz | 54 Mbps, OFDM |
| 802.11g | (none) | 2.4 GHz | 20 MHz | 54 Mbps, OFDM |
| 802.11n | Wi-Fi 4 | 2.4 / 5 GHz | 40 MHz | MIMO |
| 802.11ac | Wi-Fi 5 | 5 GHz only | 160 MHz | MU-MIMO (downlink) |
| 802.11ax | Wi-Fi 6 / **6E** | 2.4 / 5 GHz (6E adds **6 GHz**) | 160 MHz | OFDMA, BSS coloring |
| 802.11be | Wi-Fi 7 | 2.4 / 5 / 6 GHz | 320 MHz (6 GHz) | Multi-link operation |

## The three bands

| | 2.4 GHz | 5 GHz | 6 GHz |
|---|---|---|---|
| Spectrum | 2.400 - 2.4835 GHz | About 5.15 - 5.85 GHz in several U-NII sub-bands | 5.925 - 7.125 GHz (US) |
| Non-overlapping 20 MHz channels | **3 (1, 6, 11)** in the US | Up to 25 in the US (fewer if DFS channels are avoided) | **59** |
| Range / penetration | Best (lower frequency) | Shorter | Shortest |
| Congestion | Heavy: legacy clients, Bluetooth, microwaves | Moderate | Very low; only Wi-Fi 6E/7 clients |
| Security requirement | Any | Any | **WPA3 or OWE required**; no WPA2/open |

### 2.4 GHz channels

Channels are numbered every 5 MHz (channel 1 = 2412 MHz, 6 = 2437 MHz, 11 = 2462 MHz), but each transmission is about 20-22 MHz wide. Only channels that are **five channel numbers apart** avoid overlapping, which in the US (channels 1-11) leaves **1, 6, and 11**. Many other regions allow 1-13, and some designs use 1/5/9/13 there with 20 MHz OFDM-only networks, but the exam answer is 1, 6, 11.

> **Exam tip:** Any plan that uses channels such as 1, 4, 8, 11 in the same area is wrong: neighboring cells partially overlap and corrupt each other (adjacent-channel interference). Use only 1, 6, and 11 and reuse them in a honeycomb pattern.

### 5 GHz channels

| Sub-band | 20 MHz channels | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| U-NII-1 | 36, 40, 44, 48 | Always available |
| U-NII-2A | 52, 56, 60, 64 | **DFS** required |
| U-NII-2C (Extended) | 100 - 144 | **DFS** required |
| U-NII-3 | 149, 153, 157, 161, 165 | Always available (US) |

**DFS (Dynamic Frequency Selection)** means the AP must listen for radar and, if it detects radar, leave the channel. Users experience this as an AP suddenly changing channels (a brief disconnect).

### Channel width

Adjacent 20 MHz channels can be **bonded** into 40, 80, 160 (and on 6 GHz with Wi-Fi 7, 320) MHz channels.

| Wider channels give | Wider channels cost |
|---|---|
| Higher peak data rates per client | Fewer non-overlapping channels, so more co-channel interference |
| | Each doubling spreads power and adds roughly 3 dB of noise, lowering SNR |

Use **20 MHz on 2.4 GHz** (40 MHz there would consume most of the band). In dense 5 GHz deployments, 20 or 40 MHz is typical; 80 MHz is common in lighter deployments; 6 GHz has room for 80 or 160 MHz.

## RF math: dB, dBm, mW

* **dB** is a *relative* ratio between two powers (a gain or a loss).
* **dBm** is an *absolute* power level referenced to **1 mW**: 0 dBm = 1 mW.
* **dBi** is antenna gain relative to an isotropic radiator.

The **rule of 3s and 10s**:

| Change | Power |
|---|---|
| +3 dB | Double |
| -3 dB | Half |
| +10 dB | Multiply by 10 |
| -10 dB | Divide by 10 |

| dBm | mW |
|---|---|
| 30 | 1000 (1 W) |
| 23 | 200 |
| 20 | 100 |
| 17 | 50 |
| 14 | 25 |
| 10 | 10 |
| 0 | 1 |
| -10 | 0.1 |
| -70 | 0.0000001 |

Example: 17 dBm = 20 dBm - 3 dB = 100 mW / 2 = **50 mW**.

**EIRP** (effective isotropic radiated power) = transmitter power - cable/connector loss + antenna gain. An AP at 20 dBm with 2 dB of cable loss and a 6 dBi antenna radiates 20 - 2 + 6 = **24 dBm EIRP** (about 250 mW).

## Signal quality: RSSI, noise, SNR

* **RSSI** (Received Signal Strength Indicator): how strong the received signal is. Clients and APs usually express it in dBm, a negative number; closer to zero is stronger (-55 dBm is stronger than -75 dBm).
* **Noise floor**: background RF energy on the channel, typically around -90 to -95 dBm.
* **SNR** (signal-to-noise ratio) = signal - noise, in dB. With RSSI -65 dBm and noise -92 dBm, SNR = **27 dB**.

| Metric | Good target for data | Voice/video target |
|---|---|---|
| RSSI | -70 dBm or better | **-67 dBm** or better |
| SNR | 20 dB or more | **25 dB** or more |

A strong signal can still perform badly if the noise is high: RSSI -60 dBm with noise -70 dBm is only 10 dB SNR, and the client will drop to low data rates and retransmit constantly.

> **Watch out:** "Signal 38%" in Windows is a vendor-scaled percentage, not dBm. Compare like with like, and prefer readings in dBm.

## How RF behaves in buildings

| Behavior | What happens | Typical causes |
|---|---|---|
| **Absorption** | Material soaks up energy (turns it to heat), weakening the signal | Water, people, concrete, wood, drywall |
| **Reflection** | Signal bounces off a surface; reflected copies arrive later (**multipath**) | Metal: filing cabinets, elevators, ducts, metal-coated glass |
| **Refraction** | Signal bends as it passes between media of different density | Glass, water, air layers of different temperature/humidity |
| **Scattering** | Signal hits an uneven surface and breaks into many weaker reflections in many directions | Rough surfaces, chain-link fence, foliage, dust |
| **Diffraction** | Signal bends around the edge of an obstacle, leaving an RF "shadow" behind it | Building corners, large objects |

Free-space path loss also reduces signal with distance, even with nothing in the way, and higher frequencies lose more (one reason 5 and 6 GHz cells are smaller than 2.4 GHz cells).

> **Real world:** Multipath caused by reflection used to be pure trouble for 802.11a/b/g. 802.11n and later use **MIMO**, which exploits multiple paths to send parallel spatial streams.

## Interference

| Type | What it is | Effect | Fix |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Co-channel interference (CCI)** | Other APs/clients on the **same** channel within hearing range | Stations defer to each other; airtime is shared, so capacity drops (frames are not corrupted, the channel is just busy) | Better channel reuse, lower AP power, fewer SSIDs, narrower channels, more bands |
| **Adjacent-channel interference (ACI)** | APs on **overlapping** channels (e.g., 1 and 3) | Partial overlap is seen as noise; frames get corrupted, causing retries | Use only non-overlapping channels |
| **Non-Wi-Fi interference** | Devices that transmit in the same band but do not speak 802.11 | Raised noise floor, lower SNR, corrupted frames | Find with a spectrum analyzer (Cisco CleanAir), remove or move the source, change channel/band |
| **Hidden node** | Two clients can hear the AP but not each other | Their transmissions collide at the AP | RTS/CTS, AP placement, more power on clients |

Common non-Wi-Fi sources:

* **2.4 GHz**: microwave ovens (around 2.45 GHz), Bluetooth (frequency hopping across the band), cordless phones, baby monitors, wireless video cameras, Zigbee devices.
* **5 GHz**: radar (forces DFS channel changes), some cordless phones, wireless video bridges.

> **Exam tip:** "Users in the break room lose Wi-Fi every day around noon" is a microwave oven on 2.4 GHz. The fix is to move those clients to 5 GHz or relocate the AP, not to add more APs on channel 6.

Cisco controllers automate channel and power choices with **Radio Resource Management (RRM)**: Dynamic Channel Assignment (DCA) and Transmit Power Control (TPC) continuously adjust to reduce CCI and coverage holes.

## Key takeaways

* 2.4 GHz: longest range, only **1, 6, 11** non-overlapping in the US, crowded with non-Wi-Fi devices.
* 5 GHz: many channels, DFS on U-NII-2 sub-bands (radar can force channel changes).
* 6 GHz: Wi-Fi 6E/7 only, 59 20-MHz channels, **WPA3/OWE required**.
* Wider channels raise peak speed but reduce the number of usable channels and lower SNR.
* 0 dBm = 1 mW; +3 dB doubles, +10 dB multiplies by 10. EIRP = Tx power - loss + antenna gain.
* SNR = signal - noise; aim for -67 dBm RSSI and 25 dB SNR for voice.
* Absorption weakens, reflection bounces (multipath), refraction bends through media, scattering spreads, diffraction bends around edges.
* CCI = same channel sharing airtime; ACI = overlapping channels corrupting frames; non-Wi-Fi sources raise the noise floor.
