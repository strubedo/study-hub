## What compliance means here

Compliance is meeting obligations that come from outside the security team: laws, regulations, contracts and industry standards, plus the organization's own policies. An effective compliance program has four parts this objective names: people are **trained** on their obligations, adherence is **monitored** and evidenced, there are understood **consequences** of failure, and specific **privacy** and **legal** requirements are built into how data is handled.

## Compliance training

General security awareness is covered in 5.6. Compliance training is about specific legal duties:

- **Data handling:** how to classify, label, store, share, retain and dispose of data according to policy and law (Domain 3.3), including personal data, card data and health data. SP 800-53 AT-2 says literacy training content "addresses the need for operations security and the handling of personally identifiable information".
- **Anti-money laundering / counter-terrorism financing (AML/CTF):** recognizing and reporting suspicious transactions, customer due diligence ("know your customer") and record-keeping. The Financial Action Task Force (FATF) is the intergovernmental body that sets the international AML/CTF standards (the FATF Recommendations), which countries turn into national law; banks, payment firms and other regulated businesses must train their staff.
- **Anti-bribery:** not offering, paying, requesting or accepting bribes, including through agents; rules on gifts, hospitality and facilitation payments. Examples of laws: the U.S. Foreign Corrupt Practices Act and the UK Bribery Act 2010. This links to vendor-selection conflicts of interest (5.3).

Training is role-based: everyone gets data handling; finance and sales get AML and anti-bribery; developers get secure handling of production data.

## Compliance monitoring: attestations and acknowledgements

- **Acknowledgement:** an individual confirms they have received, read and will follow a document. SP 800-53 PL-4 requires "a documented acknowledgment from such individuals, indicating that they have read, understand, and agree to abide by the rules of behavior, before authorizing access", and re-acknowledgment when the rules change. Electronic signatures and check boxes count.
- **Attestation:** a formal declaration that requirements **are being met**, made by someone accountable for it. ISO/IEC 17000 (via the NIST glossary): "the issue of a statement, based on a decision, that fulfillment of specified requirements has been demonstrated." Examples: a department head attests each quarter that access reviews were completed; executives attest to the accuracy of financial controls; a vendor provides an annual compliance attestation; a PCI DSS merchant submits an Attestation of Compliance.

The difference: an acknowledgement says "I know the rule"; an attestation says "the rule is being followed, and I vouch for it". Monitoring also includes automated checks (configuration compliance scanning, DLP reports), internal audits (5.5) and tracking training completion.

## Consequences of non-compliance

| Consequence | Example |
|---|---|
| **Reputational damage** | Customers leave after a publicized breach or a regulator's public finding |
| **Financial** | Regulatory fines (the GDPR's upper tier is up to €20 million or 4% of total worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher, Article 83), breach costs, higher insurance premiums |
| **Legal** | Lawsuits, class actions, criminal liability for individuals under bribery or AML law |
| **Contractual** | Breach of contract, penalties, termination; losing the ability to process card payments if PCI DSS obligations are not met |
| **Sanctions** | Penalties imposed by a regulator or authority: orders to stop processing, mandated audits, restrictions on doing business. (In AML contexts, "sanctions" also means economic sanctions lists that screening must check) |
| **Loss of license** | A regulator withdraws the license to operate: a bank's charter, a payment institution's authorization, a healthcare provider's accreditation |

## Privacy

The objectives use generic wording; the table maps it to the GDPR and the California Consumer Privacy Act (CCPA, as amended by the CPRA), the two laws most often used to illustrate it.

| Objective term | GDPR | CCPA |
|---|---|---|
| **Right to be forgotten** | Article 17, "right to erasure ('right to be forgotten')": erase without undue delay when, for example, the data is no longer necessary, consent is withdrawn, or the person objects and there are no overriding grounds | §1798.105, right to delete |
| **Opt-in** | Consent must be "freely given, specific, informed and unambiguous ... by a statement or by a clear affirmative action" (Article 4(11)); Recital 32: "silence, pre-ticked boxes or inactivity should not therefore constitute consent". Withdrawal must be as easy as giving consent (Article 7(3)) | Opt-in required to sell or share data of consumers under 16 |
| **Opt-out** | Right to object to direct marketing at any time (Article 21(2)–(3)) | §1798.120, right to direct a business "not to sell or share the consumer's personal information" ("Do Not Sell or Share") |
| **Data correction** | Article 16, rectification of inaccurate data without undue delay | §1798.106, right to correct |
| **Processing restrictions** | Article 18, restriction: data may be stored but otherwise processed only with consent or for legal claims, for example while accuracy is contested | §1798.121, right to limit use of sensitive personal information |
| **Processing prevention** | Article 21, right to object: the controller must stop unless it shows compelling legitimate grounds; for direct marketing it must always stop | Opt-out of sale or sharing |
| **Controller vs processor** | Controller "determines the purposes and means of the processing"; processor "processes personal data on behalf of the controller" (Article 4(7), 4(8)); a contract under Article 28 binds the processor | "Business" vs "service provider" |
| **Ownership** | (See below) | |

The **data subject** is the identified or identifiable natural person the personal data relates to (Article 4(1)). Under Article 28 the processor acts "only on documented instructions from the controller", and it "shall not engage another processor" (a **sub-processor**) "without prior specific or general written authorisation of the controller"; the same data protection obligations then pass down to that sub-processor (Article 28(2) and (4)).

"Processing prevention" is not a GDPR heading; the most defensible reading is the right to object (stop processing), contrasted with restriction (keep but freeze). Under GDPR Article 12(3), the controller must respond to requests "without undue delay and in any event within one month", extendable by two further months for complex cases.

**Ownership** in the objective most defensibly means accountability for data: the organization's **data owner** decides classification and use (Domain 3.3), and contracts must state that the customer, not the vendor, owns data held by a processor and gets it back or destroyed at exit. Privacy laws give individuals rights over their personal data rather than "ownership" in a property sense, so avoid treating the data subject as the legal owner.

> **Watch out:** The right to erasure is not absolute. Article 17(3) lets the controller keep data where processing is necessary, for example, "for compliance with a legal obligation" or "for the establishment, exercise or defence of legal claims". Data under a legal hold or a statutory retention period is not erased just because someone asks.

## Legal compliance

### Legal hold

A **legal hold** (litigation hold) is a directive to preserve all information relevant to pending or **reasonably anticipated** litigation, investigation or audit. It **suspends** normal retention schedules and disposal for that information: auto-delete rules, mailbox purges, backup rotation and media destruction stop for the covered custodians and systems. The advisory committee notes to U.S. Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 37 explain that when a party is under a duty to preserve, "intervention in the routine operation of an information system is one aspect of what is often called a 'litigation hold'". Rule 37(e) applies when electronically stored information that should have been preserved is lost because a party "failed to take reasonable steps to preserve it" and cannot be restored or replaced through additional discovery: on finding prejudice to the other side, the court may order measures "no greater than necessary to cure the prejudice"; only if the party intended to deprive the other side of the information may it presume the information was unfavorable, instruct the jury to presume so, or dismiss the case or enter a default judgment.

Typical steps: legal decides a hold is needed; identify custodians and data sources; issue a written hold notice; custodians acknowledge it; IT suspends deletion and preserves data (in-place holds in email and cloud platforms, or copies); hold notices are re-sent periodically; legal releases the hold when the matter ends, and normal retention resumes.

### Legal orders

Subpoenas, court orders, search warrants and regulatory demands can compel an organization to produce data. Have a procedure: route every demand to legal, verify it is genuine and within scope, preserve the data, produce only what is required, and record what was disclosed. Data processing agreements usually require a processor to tell the controller about such demands unless the law forbids it.

### Data retention requirements

Laws set **minimum** retention for some records (tax, financial, employment, health records; the period depends on the jurisdiction) and privacy law sets a **maximum** in principle: the GDPR's storage limitation principle (Article 5(1)(e)) says personal data is kept no longer than necessary for its purpose. The retention schedule reconciles both, and a legal hold overrides the schedule's disposal step while it is in force.

> **Exam tip:** Legal hold = **preserve and stop deletion**, even if the retention schedule says to destroy. Right to be forgotten = **delete**, unless an exception such as a legal obligation or legal claims applies.

## Try it yourself

Paper exercise. For a fictional online store, write: (1) one consent checkbox that meets GDPR opt-in rules and one footer link that meets CCPA opt-out rules; (2) a three-row retention schedule (orders, support chats, job applications) with a period and a legal reason for each; (3) the five steps you would take when legal emails "preserve everything about supplier X". Check your checkbox is unticked by default and your hold steps include custodian acknowledgement and suspending auto-deletion.

## Key takeaways

- Compliance training covers data handling, AML/CTF (FATF standards) and anti-bribery, targeted by role.
- Acknowledgement: "I have read and will follow it." Attestation: "requirements are being met, and I vouch for it."
- Consequences: reputational, financial, legal, contractual, sanctions and loss of license.
- Privacy rights: erasure (right to be forgotten), opt-in consent (no pre-ticked boxes) and opt-out (CCPA sale/sharing, direct marketing), correction, restriction, objection.
- Controller decides purposes and means; processor acts on its behalf under contract.
- Legal hold suspends normal retention and disposal for relevant data; legal orders go through legal; retention schedules balance legal minimums and privacy maximums.
