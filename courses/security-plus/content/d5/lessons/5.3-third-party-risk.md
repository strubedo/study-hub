## Third parties extend your attack surface

Every supplier that hosts your data, connects to your network or ships software you run brings its risks into your organization. NIST CSF 2.0 places this under **Govern** (GV.SC, cybersecurity supply chain risk management): suppliers are "known and prioritized by criticality" (GV.SC-04), security requirements are "integrated into contracts and other types of agreements" (GV.SC-05), "planning and due diligence are performed to reduce risks before entering into formal supplier or other third-party relationships" (GV.SC-06), supplier risks are "assessed, responded to, and monitored over the course of the relationship" (GV.SC-07), and plans cover what happens "after the conclusion of a partnership or service agreement" (GV.SC-10). NIST SP 800-161 Rev. 1 is the detailed guide. The life cycle is: select, contract, monitor, and exit.

## Vendor selection

### Solicitation documents

| Document | Purpose | When | What comes back |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Request for information (RFI)** | Learn what the market offers and who can do it | Early, while requirements are still forming | Capabilities, approaches, rough costs; no commitment |
| **Expression of interest (EOI)** | Invite suppliers to say they are interested and qualified, to build a shortlist | Early, before a formal tender | A short statement of interest and qualifications |
| **Request for proposal (RFP)** | Ask shortlisted suppliers to propose a solution to defined requirements | When requirements are known but the solution can vary | A full proposal: approach, staffing, security, price |
| **Request for quote (RFQ)** | Ask for a price for a precisely specified product or service | When you know exactly what you want | Prices, terms, delivery dates |

SP 800-161 Rev. 1 describes the sequence: during market research the enterprise "may also use a request for information (RFIs), sources sought notice (SSNs), and/or due diligence questionnaires for the initial screening", then completes procurement "by releasing a statement of work (SOW), performance work statement (PWS), or statement of objective (SOO) for the release of a request for proposal (RFP) or request for quotes (RFQ)". So: **RFI (and EOI) → requirements and SOW → RFP or RFQ → evaluation → contract**.

"Expression of interest" has no NIST definition. In public procurement it usually means a call for suppliers to register interest and basic qualifications before an RFP (the supplier's reply is also called an EOI). That is the reading used here; no question depends on the finer distinction between an EOI and an RFI.

### Due diligence

Due diligence is the investigation done **before** signing, so the decision rests on evidence rather than the sales pitch. Typical checks:

- Security posture: a completed security questionnaire, independent attestation reports (SOC 2 Type II, ISO/IEC 27001 certificate with its scope, PCI DSS Attestation of Compliance), recent penetration-test summaries, breach history, vulnerability-disclosure practice.
- Financial stability (will the vendor exist in three years?), ownership and foreign-influence concerns, sanctions screening, litigation, insurance coverage.
- Operational capability: sub-processors and fourth parties, data locations, BC/DR arrangements, references.
- Market concentration: SP 800-161 suggests considering "the market concentration for the sought-after product or service" to spot shared dependencies.

### Conflict of interest

A conflict of interest exists when someone involved in selecting or overseeing a vendor has a personal interest that could influence the decision: an evaluator who owns shares in a bidder, a manager whose relative works there, gifts or hospitality from a bidder. The control is **disclosure and recusal**: evaluators declare conflicts, conflicted people step out of scoring, and procurement records the declaration. SP 800-53's PL-4 lists conflict-of-interest agreements as a type of access agreement. Anti-bribery training (5.4) is related.

## Agreement types

| Agreement | What it is | Binding? |
|---|---|---|
| **MSA (master services agreement)** | The umbrella contract: the general terms for all future work between two parties (liability, indemnity, confidentiality, data protection, audit rights, dispute resolution, termination) | Yes |
| **SOW (statement of work)** | The specific work under the MSA: deliverables, tasks, schedule, acceptance criteria, price. NIST (IR 7622): "details what the developer must do in the performance of the contract" | Yes |
| **SLA (service-level agreement)** | Measurable service commitments and the consequences of missing them. NIST SP 800-47 Rev. 1: a commitment covering "expected performance level (e.g., reliability, acceptable quality, and response times), and requirements for reporting, resolution, and termination" | Yes (as part of the contract) |
| **SLO (service-level objective)** | A target value for one measured aspect of service. Google's SRE book: "a target value or range of values for a service level that is measured by an SLI" | Usually internal; becomes contractual only inside an SLA |
| **MOU (memorandum of understanding)** | A statement of mutual intent to work together; CNSSI 4009: "a mutual understanding ... that does not contain an expectation of payment, and under which the parties do not rely on each other to execute or deliver on any responsibilities" | Usually not legally binding |
| **MOA (memorandum of agreement)** | Documents specific responsibilities and terms the parties agree to carry out, possibly with reimbursement (CNSSI 4009) | More formal; can be binding depending on wording |
| **NDA (non-disclosure agreement)** | Delineates "specific information, materials, or knowledge that the signatories agree not to release or divulge to any other parties" (SP 800-47 Rev. 1). Mutual or one-way | Yes |

Distinctions that decide exam questions:

- **SLA vs SLO vs SLI:** the SLI (service-level indicator) is the measurement (latency, error rate, availability percentage). The SLO is the target ("99.9% monthly availability"). The SLA is the agreement that contains SLOs **and consequences**: the SRE book's test is "what happens if the SLOs aren't met? If there is no explicit consequence, then you are almost certainly looking at an SLO." Service credits or penalties mean SLA.
- **MOU vs MOA:** both are memoranda. Intent and goals with no reliance on each other: MOU. Specific responsibilities each party will carry out: MOA. Neither normally replaces a commercial contract.
- **MSA vs SOW:** the MSA is negotiated once and sets the legal terms; each new project gets its own SOW under it. If the question asks where the deliverables and acceptance criteria for "phase 2" go, it is a SOW.

> **Exam tip:** Put security requirements (breach notification within a set time, encryption, sub-processor approval, right to audit, return or destruction of data at exit) in the **MSA** so they apply to every SOW.

## Vendor monitoring

Due diligence is a snapshot; monitoring keeps it current:

- **Right to audit:** a contract clause that lets the customer (or an auditor it appoints) audit the vendor's controls, records and facilities, usually with notice and scope limits. Without it, you get only what the vendor chooses to share. Large cloud providers usually offer their third-party reports instead of individual customer audits.
- **Service-level monitoring:** measure actual performance against the SLA (your own monitoring, not just the vendor's monthly report), claim credits, and escalate repeated misses.
- **Vendor registry:** the inventory of all third parties: what they provide, what data and access they have, criticality tier, contract dates, owner, assessment status. It answers "who has our data?" during an incident and drives reassessment schedules.
- **Vendor assessment:** periodic re-evaluation, tiered by criticality: questionnaires for low-risk vendors, document review and interviews for medium, on-site or deep technical assessment for critical ones. SP 800-53 SR-6 (supplier assessments and reviews) is the matching control.
- **Compliance attestation:** formal statements that the vendor meets a standard: a SOC 2 Type II report (an independent CPA examination over a period), a PCI DSS Attestation of Compliance, an ISO/IEC 27001 certificate, or a signed annual self-attestation. Read the scope and exceptions, not just the cover page.
- **Penetration testing:** require the vendor to have independent tests done and share the results, or obtain written permission to test the vendor's service yourself. Testing a provider without permission can breach its terms and the law.

## Limitations and constraints

Third-party oversight is never complete. The constraints the objectives list, with what each means in practice:

| Constraint | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Staffing limitations** | Too few people to assess every vendor deeply; forces tiering |
| **Resource availability** | Budget, tools and time limit how many assessments and audits happen |
| **Environment** | Cloud and multi-tenant environments cannot be inspected physically or tested freely; the vendor's own environment and change pace limit what you can verify |
| **Legal and regulatory factors** | Regulations may require specific clauses or rule out some vendors; privacy law limits what data a vendor may receive |
| **Geography/jurisdiction** | Data location and the vendor's home country decide which laws apply and whether data can be compelled by a foreign government (data sovereignty, 3.1) |
| **Financial/return on investment (ROI)** | Assessment and contract effort must be proportionate to the vendor's risk and spend |
| **Vendor lock-in** | Proprietary formats or deep integration make leaving costly, weakening your leverage to demand fixes; plan exit and data portability up front |
| **Assurance mechanisms** | Each source of assurance has limits: a SOC 2 covers a defined scope and period and may exclude sub-service organizations; a questionnaire is self-reported; a certificate says a management system exists, not that every control works |

## Rules of engagement

When you assess or test a vendor (or a vendor tests you), the **rules of engagement (ROE)** define what is allowed. NIST SP 800-115 defines ROE as "detailed guidelines and constraints regarding the execution of information security testing", established before testing starts, giving the test team "authority to conduct defined activities without the need for additional permissions". Its template covers purpose and scope, assumptions and limitations, risks, personnel and points of contact, the test schedule and hours, the test site, equipment and tools, the communication strategy, incident handling (criteria for halting testing), the target systems with authorized addresses and an **exclude list**, and allowable and unallowable activities. For vendor audits the same idea applies: agreed dates, scope, evidence-handling and confidentiality rules, and who may be interviewed.

## Try it yourself

Paper exercise. Match each need to a document: (1) Contoso wants to know which managed-SOC providers support its SIEM, without committing; (2) the legal terms that will govern every future project with the chosen provider; (3) the deliverables, schedule and acceptance criteria for onboarding 200 log sources; (4) a 15-minute response time for critical alerts with service credits if missed; (5) the provider's internal target of triaging 95% of alerts within 10 minutes; (6) a promise not to disclose Contoso's network diagrams during the bid. Answers: RFI, MSA, SOW, SLA, SLO, NDA.

## Key takeaways

- Selection: RFI and EOI to learn and shortlist; SOW defines the work; RFP for proposals, RFQ for prices on a fixed spec. Due diligence before signing; conflicts of interest disclosed and recused.
- MSA = umbrella terms; SOW = the specific work; SLA = measurable commitments with consequences; SLO = target; SLI = measurement; MOU = intent, usually non-binding; MOA = specific responsibilities; NDA = confidentiality.
- Monitoring: right-to-audit clause, service-level monitoring, vendor registry, periodic assessments, compliance attestations and penetration testing.
- Constraints: staffing, resources, environment, legal/regulatory, jurisdiction, ROI, lock-in and the limits of each assurance mechanism.
- Rules of engagement set scope, timing, contacts, allowed activities, exclusions and stop conditions before any assessment or test.
