## What a penetration test is

NIST SP 800-115 defines penetration testing as "security testing in which evaluators mimic real-world attacks in an attempt to identify ways to circumvent the security features of an application, system, or network", often "issuing real attacks on real systems and data". SP 800-53 Rev. 5 (CA-8) adds that it "goes beyond automated vulnerability scanning", is done by people with demonstrable skills, and that "all parties agree to the rules of engagement before commencing". A vulnerability scan says a weakness **may** exist; a penetration test tries to **exploit** it and shows what an attacker could reach.

## The four phases (SP 800-115)

SP 800-115 presents a four-stage methodology (and notes it is one acceptable way to group the work):

1. **Planning:** "rules are identified, management approval is finalized and documented, and testing goals are set." No testing happens. This is where the scope, the rules of engagement (ROE) and written authorization are produced.
2. **Discovery:** first, information gathering and scanning (port and service identification, DNS and WHOIS queries, banner grabbing, employee names from public sites); second, **vulnerability analysis**, comparing what was found with vulnerability databases and the testers' knowledge.
3. **Attack:** verify potential vulnerabilities by exploiting them: gain access, escalate privileges, browse for more information, install additional tools. Each new foothold loops back to more discovery.
4. **Reporting:** happens "simultaneously with the other three phases": the plan in planning, logs during discovery and attack, and the final report with findings, risk and mitigations.

Industry methodologies (PTES, OWASP's testing guide) slice the work differently, but the order is the same: authorize and plan, gather information, find weaknesses, exploit, report.

## Rules of engagement and authorization

Testing without written authorization is an attack. The ROE (SP 800-115 Appendix B, also covered in 5.3) records: purpose and **scope** (targets, authorized IP ranges and the **exclude list**), assumptions and limitations, risks, personnel and points of contact, the **schedule and permitted hours**, test site and equipment, the communication plan, **incident handling and criteria for halting the test**, and the **allowable and unallowable activities** (for example: no denial of service, no social engineering of the help desk, no changes to production data). If a cloud or hosting provider is involved, its testing policy must permit the test.

## Knowledge of the environment

| SY0-801 term | Older term | What testers are given | Simulates | Trade-off |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Known environment** | White box | Full information: network diagrams, source code, credentials, configurations | An insider or an attacker who has already done extensive research | Most thorough coverage in the time; least realistic for an outside attacker |
| **Partially known environment** | Gray box | Some information: an ordinary user account, the in-scope address ranges, application documentation | An attacker with a foothold or a malicious user | Balance of realism and efficiency; the most common choice |
| **Unknown environment** | Black box | Nothing beyond the target's name or a starting point | An external attacker with no inside knowledge | Most realistic; time is spent on reconnaissance, so coverage is lower |

SP 800-115 Appendix C uses the older white, black and gray box terms for application testing (white box = analysis of source code; black box = testing the running application; gray box = a combination). CompTIA uses the known/unknown/partially known wording.

Separately, SP 800-115 distinguishes **overt** testing ("performed with the knowledge and consent of the organization's IT staff") from **covert** testing (performed without the knowledge of IT staff, but with management's authorization), which also tests detection and response.

## Types of penetration testing

- **Physical:** testing physical controls: tailgating, badge cloning, lock picking, entering secured areas, planting a device. SP 800-115 mentions dumpster diving and physical walkthroughs as discovery techniques, and CA-8 says tests "can exercise both physical and technical controls". Physical tests need especially explicit ROE (a signed authorization letter carried by testers, a contact who can confirm it to security staff or police).
- **Offensive (red team):** testers act as the adversary. CNSSI 4009: a red team is "authorized and organized to emulate a potential adversary's attack or exploitation capabilities against an enterprise's security posture". SP 800-53 CA-8(2) is the red-team exercise enhancement.
- **Defensive (blue team):** the defenders: CNSSI 4009's blue team is "the group responsible for defending an enterprise's use of information systems by maintaining its security posture against a group of mock attackers". Defensive testing checks whether monitoring, detection and response work.
- **Integrated (purple team):** red and blue work together, sharing what was attempted and what was seen in real time, so detections are tuned and gaps closed during the exercise instead of in a report weeks later. "Purple team" has no NIST glossary entry; this is the common industry meaning and the most defensible reading of CompTIA's "integrated".

A **white team** (CNSSI 4009) referees exercises and enforces the rules.

> **Exam tip:** "Emulate a known threat group's techniques and see how far you get" = offensive/red. "Measure whether the SOC detects and contains the activity" = defensive/blue. "Both teams in the same room, replaying each technique until it is detected" = integrated/purple.

## Reconnaissance

| | Passive reconnaissance | Active reconnaissance |
|---|---|---|
| Definition (SP 800-115 terms) | "Does not involve any direct interaction with the targets" | "Involves direct interaction with a target, such as sending packets" |
| Examples | OSINT: search engines, company website and job ads, social media, public DNS records through third-party services, WHOIS, certificate transparency logs, breach dumps, passive wireless sniffing | Port and service scans, ping sweeps, banner grabbing, vulnerability scans, directory brute-forcing, calling the help desk |
| Detectable by the target? | Generally no | Yes: logs, IDS alerts |
| Needs authorization? | Should still be in scope and lawful | Yes, always in the ROE |

> **Watch out:** Querying the target's **own** DNS servers or web site is direct interaction, so purists class it as active. Looking up the same records through a third-party service or archive is passive. Exam questions usually make the distinction obvious: sending packets to the target is active.

## After the test

The report should rank findings by risk, give evidence (screenshots, commands, timestamps), explain the business impact, recommend fixes, and include an executive summary. Testers clean up: remove accounts, tools and files they created, and confirm with the system owner. Findings go into the vulnerability management process (Domain 4.3 lists penetration test report review as a prioritization input) and, where they reveal systemic risk, into the risk register (5.2). Retest after remediation.

## Try it yourself

Paper exercise. Write a one-page ROE for testing a fictional Contoso web store at 203.0.113.10–203.0.113.20: state the environment type (known, partially known or unknown) and why; list two allowed and three forbidden activities; give the testing window; name the stop conditions; add an exclude list (the payment provider's systems). Then sort these reconnaissance tasks into passive and active: reading Contoso's job postings, an Nmap SYN scan of the range, searching certificate transparency logs for contoso subdomains, grabbing the HTTP banner, reviewing LinkedIn for staff names.

Answers for the sort: passive: job postings, certificate transparency, LinkedIn. Active: SYN scan, banner grab.

## Key takeaways

- A penetration test exploits weaknesses to show real impact; a vulnerability scan only flags them.
- SP 800-115 phases: planning (scope, ROE, approval; no testing), discovery (information gathering, scanning, vulnerability analysis), attack (exploit, escalate, loop back to discovery), reporting (throughout).
- Known = white box (full information), partially known = gray box, unknown = black box (no information).
- Physical tests target facilities and people; offensive = red team; defensive = blue team; integrated = purple team.
- Passive reconnaissance does not touch the target; active reconnaissance interacts with it and needs explicit authorization.
- Written authorization and ROE (scope, exclusions, hours, contacts, stop criteria) come before any testing.
