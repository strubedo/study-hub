## The risk management cycle

NIST SP 800-39 describes risk management as four components: **frame** risk (set the context: assumptions, constraints, risk tolerance, priorities), **assess** it, **respond** to it, and **monitor** it over time. SP 800-30 Rev. 1 then breaks the assessment into four steps: prepare for the assessment, conduct it, communicate the results, and maintain it. The SY0-801 objective follows the same flow with its own words: identification, assessment, analysis, the register, treatment. This lesson covers the first three and the loss formulas; the next covers the register, treatment and business-level considerations.

## Risk identification

Risk is a function of the likelihood that a threat exploits a vulnerability and the impact if it does (SP 800-30). You cannot assess risk to things you have not identified, so identification starts with:

- **Asset identification:** what the organization has and what it is worth. Hardware, software, data sets, services, people and facilities (Domain 4.2 covers inventory). NIST IR 8286 lists "identification of the organization's mission-supporting assets and their valuation" as the first input to cybersecurity risk identification, followed by threats, vulnerabilities and the resulting risk scenarios.
- **Stakeholder ownership:** every asset and every risk needs a named owner who is accountable. IR 8286 defines the **risk owner** as "the designated party responsible and accountable for ensuring that the risk is maintained in accordance with enterprise requirements", who may delegate day-to-day work to a risk manager. Owners are usually business leaders, not IT staff: the head of payroll owns the risk of payroll fraud even though IT runs the system.

Then you list **risk scenarios**: a threat source, an event, the vulnerability or condition it exploits, the asset affected and the consequence. "A phishing email harvests an accounts-payable clerk's credentials and an attacker changes a supplier's bank details" is a scenario; "phishing" on its own is not.

## Risk assessment: scoring and categorization

- **Scoring:** give each scenario a likelihood and an impact and combine them. With a 1–5 scale for each, risk = likelihood × impact gives 1–25, which can be binned into low, medium, high and critical. IR 8286 calls this combination the **exposure** rating; SP 800-30 and ISO 31000 call it the **level of risk**.
- **Categorization:** group risks so they can be compared, routed to the right owner and rolled up: by type (strategic, operational, financial, compliance, reputational), by security objective (confidentiality, integrity, availability), by business unit or by system. In U.S. federal practice, systems are also categorized by impact level (low, moderate, high) under FIPS 199 before controls are selected (SP 800-37 Rev. 2, the "Categorize" step of the RMF).

## Risk analysis

For each risk the analysis records:

| Element | What it captures |
|---|---|
| **Impact** | What happens if the event occurs: money, downtime, data exposed, safety, legal and reputational consequences |
| **Likelihood / probability** | How likely the event is over a period: a qualitative level, a percentage, or an annual rate |
| **Owner** | Who is accountable for the risk and for deciding the response |
| **Current mitigations** | Controls already in place, which reduce likelihood or impact today and so set the current level of risk |

### Qualitative vs quantitative

SP 800-30 Rev. 1 describes three approaches:

| Approach | How values are expressed | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Qualitative** | Non-numerical levels (very low, low, moderate, high, very high) | Fast; easy to communicate to decision makers | Small range of values makes ranking hard; different experts give different answers unless levels are clearly defined |
| **Quantitative** | Numbers whose meaning and proportionality hold both inside and outside the assessment (dollars, events per year) | Best support for **cost-benefit analysis** of responses | Needs good data and expert effort; false precision if subjective guesses are buried in the numbers |
| **Semi-quantitative** | Bins or scales (1–10, 0–100) that translate into qualitative terms | Combines easy communication with relative ranking | Expert judgment is still behind every value |

> **Exam tip:** A heat map or "high/medium/low" is **qualitative**. Anything producing a dollar figure per year (ALE) is **quantitative**. A 1–25 score from 1–5 scales is semi-quantitative in NIST's terms, but exam questions usually treat any non-monetary score as qualitative.

## Quantitative loss formulas: SLE, ALE, ARO

IR 8286A notes that leaders may want impact as "the loss for each occurrence (the single loss expectancy, or SLE)" or as "the total loss for that risk over an annual period (the annualized loss expectancy, or ALE)". The formulas:

| Term | Meaning | Formula |
|---|---|---|
| **Asset value (AV)** | The value of the asset at risk, including replacement, lost revenue and data value | Given or estimated |
| **Exposure factor (EF)** | The fraction of the asset value lost in one occurrence (0–100%) | Estimated |
| **Single loss expectancy (SLE)** | Expected loss from one occurrence | **SLE = AV × EF** |
| **Annualized rate of occurrence (ARO)** | Expected occurrences per year | Once every 4 years = 0.25; twice a year = 2 |
| **Annualized loss expectancy (ALE)** | Expected loss per year | **ALE = SLE × ARO** |

### Worked example 1: a single risk

Contoso's order database and the revenue it supports are valued at **$400,000**. A ransomware event would cost about **25%** of that value (recovery effort, lost orders, overtime). Contoso's history and industry data suggest one such event every **5 years**.

- SLE = $400,000 × 0.25 = **$100,000**
- ARO = 1 ÷ 5 = **0.2**
- ALE = $100,000 × 0.2 = **$20,000 per year**

### Worked example 2: is a control worth it?

An immutable backup service with automated recovery costs **$9,000 per year**. Contoso estimates it would cut the exposure factor from 25% to 5% (the data comes back quickly), while the ARO stays at 0.2, because backups do not stop the attack.

- New SLE = $400,000 × 0.05 = $20,000; new ALE = $20,000 × 0.2 = **$4,000**
- Reduction in ALE = $20,000 − $4,000 = $16,000 per year
- Net benefit = $16,000 − $9,000 = **$7,000 per year**, so the control is cost-justified.

The rule: a control is worth buying, on purely financial grounds, when the **ALE before − ALE after − annual cost of the control > 0**.

### Worked example 3: frequent, small losses

Lost or stolen laptops: each costs **$2,500** in hardware plus **$1,500** in staff time and re-imaging (full-disk encryption means no data breach), so SLE = **$4,000**. Contoso loses about **6 per year**: ALE = $4,000 × 6 = **$24,000**. A frequent minor event can have a higher ALE than a rare major one, so frequency matters as much as the size of each loss (IR 8286A also stresses consistent documentation of impact frequency so that risk registers can be combined and compared).

> **Watch out:** ARO is a rate, not a probability, and can be greater than 1. "Once every 20 years" is 0.05, not 20. Always convert the stated interval before multiplying, and keep the units (dollars per event, events per year, dollars per year).

> **Real world:** Quantitative numbers are estimates. Record where AV, EF and ARO came from (insurance claims data, incident history, industry reports) so reviewers can challenge them; IR 8286 warns that quantification done ad hoc is "sometimes not performed with the same rigor" as other risk work.

## Try it yourself

Paper exercise. Compute SLE and ALE for each risk, rank them by ALE, and decide whether the control is cost-justified:

| Risk | AV | EF | Frequency | Control and annual cost | Effect of control |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A. Web store DDoS | $300,000 | 10% | 3 per year | DDoS protection service, $40,000 | EF to 2% |
| B. Data center flood | $2,000,000 | 60% | once in 50 years | Move servers to an upper floor, $10,000/year (amortized) | EF to 5% |
| C. BEC payment fraud | $150,000 | 100% | once in 2 years | Call-back verification procedure, $5,000 | ARO to 0.1 |

Answers: A: SLE $30,000, ALE $90,000; after: $6,000 × 3 = $18,000; saving $72,000 − $40,000 = $32,000, justified. B: SLE $1,200,000, ARO 0.02, ALE $24,000; after: $100,000 × 0.02 = $2,000; saving $22,000 − $10,000 = $12,000, justified. C: SLE $150,000, ARO 0.5, ALE $75,000; after: $15,000; saving $60,000 − $5,000 = $55,000, justified. Ranking by ALE: A, C, B.

## Key takeaways

- Identification starts with assets (and their value) and a named owner for each asset and risk; then write specific risk scenarios.
- Assessment scores risks (likelihood × impact) and categorizes them so they can be compared and routed.
- Analysis records impact, likelihood, owner and current mitigations.
- Qualitative uses levels and communicates well; quantitative uses numbers and supports cost-benefit decisions; semi-quantitative uses scales or bins.
- SLE = AV × EF; ALE = SLE × ARO; ARO is events per year (once in 4 years = 0.25).
- A control is financially justified when the drop in ALE exceeds its annual cost.
