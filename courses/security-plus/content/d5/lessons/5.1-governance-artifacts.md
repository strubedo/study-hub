## Why the document type matters

Governance turns leadership's intent into rules people can follow and auditors can check. On the exam the question is usually "which document is this?" or "which document should change?", and the answer depends on three properties: **how binding** it is, **how specific** it is, and **who approves** it. NIST SP 800-12 Rev. 1 (section 5.1) gives the backbone used here: policy is written at a broad level; **standards** "specify uniform use of specific technologies, parameters, or procedures" and "are normally compulsory"; **guidelines** help people secure systems where a standard is "not always achievable, appropriate, or cost-effective" and can be implemented correctly "in more than one way"; **procedures** "describe how to implement applicable security policies, standards, and guidelines" as detailed steps.

| Artifact | Answers | Mandatory? | Specificity | Typically approved by | Review |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Policy** | What must be achieved, and why; who is responsible | Yes | Broad, technology-neutral | Senior management or the board (often on the CISO's recommendation) | Annually and after major events |
| **Standard** | Which specific technology, setting or value everyone must use | Yes | Specific, measurable | CISO or a security governance committee, under the policy's authority | When technology or threats change |
| **Baseline** | The minimum secure configuration for one class of system | Yes (deviations need an exception) | Very specific: setting by setting | Security architecture or engineering, under a standard | Each new OS/product version |
| **Guideline** | Recommended practice; how to do it well | No (discretionary) | Advisory | The security team or a subject-matter owner | As needed |
| **Procedure (SOP, runbook)** | Exactly how to do a task, step by step | Yes, for whoever performs the task | Step by step | The process or system owner | When the system or process changes |
| **Plan (BCP, DRP)** | Who does what, with which resources, when a defined situation happens | Yes, once invoked | Organized around a scenario | Executive sponsor or a continuity steering group | After tests, exercises and real use |

The approver column is typical practice, not a rule from a single standard: every organization sets its own approval chain in its governance charter. What does not vary is the direction of authority: a standard must trace back to a policy, a baseline to a standard, and a procedure must not contradict any of them. NIST SP 800-53 Rev. 5 puts this into every control family's first control (AC-1, AT-1, IR-1 and so on): develop, document and disseminate a policy that addresses "purpose, scope, roles, responsibilities, management commitment, coordination among organizational entities, and compliance", plus procedures to implement it, and review both on a defined frequency. It adds a useful warning: "Simply restating controls does not constitute an organizational policy or procedure."

> **Exam tip:** Must + broad = **policy**. Must + a specific value (AES-256, 14-character passwords, a named product) = **standard**. Should / recommended = **guideline**. Numbered steps = **procedure**. A per-system list of settings = **baseline**. A scenario-driven "when X happens, these people do these things" = **plan**.

## Guidelines

- **Benchmarks:** consensus secure-configuration recommendations for a product, such as the CIS Benchmarks (developed by a community of experts for operating systems, cloud platforms, network devices and applications). A benchmark is guidance until the organization adopts it; once security says "all Windows servers must meet CIS Level 1 except where an exception is approved", the benchmark has become a **baseline** under a **standard**.
- **Advisories:** notices about a specific threat or vulnerability and what to do about it: CISA cybersecurity advisories, vendor security advisories, CERT notes. They inform priorities (Domain 4.3) but bind no one by themselves.
- **Implementation guides:** product-specific instructions for configuring something securely. The DISA **Security Technical Implementation Guides (STIGs)** are the best-known; the NIST glossary describes a STIG as an "implementation guide geared to a specific product and version". STIGs are mandatory for U.S. DoD systems because DoD policy says so; for everyone else they are guidance.
- **Reference architecture:** a vetted template design (for example, a cloud landing zone, a Zero Trust layout or a three-tier web design) that teams start from instead of inventing their own.

## Standards

The objectives list five kinds of standard. Note that "standard" covers both **organizational standards** (internal rules) and **external standards** that an organization adopts:

- **Baselines:** minimum configurations per system type (a hardened image, a Group Policy baseline, a firewall template). SP 800-53B calls its low/moderate/high control sets "control baselines", the same idea at the control level.
- **Passwords:** length, complexity (or the lack of it, per SP 800-63B-4), screening against breached-password lists, MFA requirements, rotation rules, storage (salted, slow hashing; Domain 1.3).
- **Physical security:** badge types, visitor escort rules, camera retention periods, lock grades, data-center access tiers.
- **Request for comments (RFC):** the IETF's document series. Some RFCs are Internet Standards, others are informational or experimental; an organizational standard can require them ("TLS 1.3 per RFC 9846, which replaced RFC 8446 in 2026; DMARC per RFC 9989, which replaced RFC 7489 in May 2026, with p=reject").
- **Encryption:** approved algorithms, key lengths, modes and protocols (AES-256-GCM, RSA 3072 or ECDSA P-256 minimum, TLS 1.2 minimum with 1.3 preferred, FIPS 140-3 validated modules), plus key management rules.

## Procedures

- **Standard operating procedure (SOP):** a documented, repeatable procedure for a routine task so everyone does it the same way: onboarding a user, granting firewall access, handling a returned laptop, rotating a certificate.
- **Runbook:** the detailed, usually technical, steps for one operational task on a specific system, often written so it can be scripted or automated: "restore the CRM database from the immutable snapshot", "fail over the VPN cluster".
- **Playbook** (named in Domain 4.7, not here): the response strategy for a type of event, such as ransomware or phishing, including decisions and escalation, which calls runbooks for the technical steps.

These three words have no single authoritative definition; the distinctions above are common practice and match how CompTIA groups them (SOPs and runbooks under procedures, playbooks under incident response).

## Plans

- **Business continuity plan (BCP):** how the organization keeps essential functions running during and after a disruption (manual workarounds, alternate staff and sites, communications).
- **Disaster recovery plan (DRP):** how IT restores systems and data after a disaster, to meet the RTO and RPO set in the business impact analysis.

Both were covered in Domain 3.4 (sites, backups, RTO/RPO/MTD and test types); here, remember that they are **governance artifacts**: approved, owned, distributed, tested and kept current like any other document.

## Policies

| Policy | What it governs | Example statement |
|---|---|---|
| **Information security** | The program itself: scope, objectives, roles, authority for all other documents | "The CISO is responsible for maintaining standards that implement this policy." |
| **Acceptable use (AUP)** | What users may and may not do with organizational systems and data | "Corporate email must not be used to run a personal business." |
| **Bring your own device (BYOD)** | Using personal devices for work: enrolment, separation of work data, remote wipe scope, privacy | "Personal phones may access email only after enrolment in the work profile." |
| **Clean desk** | Leaving no sensitive information or unlocked devices unattended | "Lock screens when away; clear desks of confidential papers at end of day." |
| **Incident response** | Authority, roles, reporting duties and severity scheme for incidents; the IR plan implements it | "All employees must report suspected incidents to the service desk at once." |
| **Data classification and retention** | Levels, labeling, handling per level, how long each record type is kept | "Customer contracts are Confidential and kept 7 years after expiry." |
| **Access control** | Least privilege, need to know, approval of access, reviews, privileged access | "Access is granted by role and reviewed quarterly by the data owner." |
| **Data disposal** | How data and media are destroyed when retention ends (SP 800-88 clear/purge/destroy) | "Failed SSDs are shredded; certificates of destruction are kept." |
| **Vulnerability disclosure** | How outside researchers may report flaws, what testing is authorized, how reports are handled | "We will not pursue legal action for good-faith research that follows this policy." |
| **Privacy** | How personal data is collected, used, shared and protected, and data subject rights (Domain 5.4) | "We process customer data only for the purposes stated at collection." |

PL-4 in SP 800-53 (rules of behavior) requires a **documented acknowledgment** that individuals "have read, understand, and agree to abide by" the rules before they get access; an AUP signed at onboarding is the everyday example, and it ties directly to the compliance monitoring in 5.4. For vulnerability disclosure, CISA's Binding Operational Directive 20-01 (September 2020) required U.S. federal civilian agencies to publish a VDP that authorizes good-faith research by the general public and does not demand personal information from reporters; it is a good model for any organization.

> **Watch out:** A policy that names a product or a setting has drifted into standard territory and will need rewriting every time technology changes. Keep policies short and stable; put specifics in standards and baselines.

> **Real world:** When an auditor asks for "the policy", they also want evidence that it is approved (a signature or minutes), communicated (acknowledgments, training records) and reviewed on schedule (version history). An unsigned draft on a file share is not a policy.

## Try it yourself

Paper exercise. Classify each statement as policy, standard, baseline, guideline, procedure or plan, then say who should approve it:

1. "Administrative access to servers requires MFA."
2. "Use phishing-resistant authenticators (FIDO2 keys) for all administrator accounts."
3. "On ws-class laptops, set the screen lock to 5 minutes and disable USB mass storage."
4. "When writing scripts, prefer the built-in secrets manager to environment variables."
5. "1. Open a ticket. 2. Verify the requester's manager approval. 3. Add the user to the group. 4. Record the change."
6. "If the primary data center is unavailable for more than 4 hours, the DR coordinator declares a disaster and moves payroll to the alternate site."

Answers: 1 policy (senior management); 2 standard (CISO or governance committee); 3 baseline (security engineering); 4 guideline (security team); 5 procedure/SOP (process owner); 6 plan (continuity steering group).

## Key takeaways

- Policy: mandatory, broad, approved at the top. Standard: mandatory, specific. Baseline: mandatory minimum configuration. Guideline: recommended. Procedure: mandatory step-by-step instructions for a task. Plan: what to do in a defined situation.
- Guidelines include benchmarks, advisories, implementation guides (STIGs) and reference architectures; adopting one as required turns it into a standard or baseline.
- Standards cover baselines, passwords, physical security, RFCs and encryption.
- SOPs document routine tasks; runbooks give technical steps for a specific system; playbooks (4.7) orchestrate a response.
- BCP keeps the business running; DRP restores IT.
- Know the ten listed policies by their purpose, and that SP 800-53's "-1" controls require each family's policy and procedures, reviewed on a schedule.
- Every artifact needs an owner, approval, communication (acknowledgment) and scheduled review.
