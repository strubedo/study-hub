## From analysis to decisions

The previous lesson produced scored, owned risks. This one covers where they are recorded (the register), what can be done about each (treatment), and the business context that decides which treatment is acceptable.

## The risk register

NIST IR 8286 quotes OMB Circular A-11: a risk register is "a repository of risk information including the data understood about risks over time", and says it "provides a formal communication vehicle for sharing and coordinating cybersecurity risk activities". IR 8286's notional register has these columns: ID, priority, risk description, risk category, current assessment (likelihood, impact and exposure rating), risk response type, risk response cost, risk response description, **risk owner** and status. Many organizations add the current controls, a **residual risk** rating after the planned response, a target residual risk, and review dates.

| ID | Risk | Category | L | I | Score | Owner | Current mitigations | Response | Residual | Next review |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| R-07 | Supplier bank-detail change via BEC | Financial | 4 | 4 | 16 | CFO | Email filtering | Mitigate: call-back verification | 6 | 2027-02 |
| R-12 | Ransomware on file servers | Operational | 3 | 5 | 15 | COO | EDR, nightly backups | Mitigate + transfer (cyber insurance) | 8 | 2027-01 |

The two bullets the objectives name:

- **Communication:** the register is how risk information moves upward (rolled up into an enterprise risk profile for executives and the board) and sideways (owners, control teams, auditors). IR 8286 says it should become "the ordinary means of communicating risk information".
- **Reviews:** risk changes, so the register is reviewed on a schedule (quarterly is common) and after triggering events: a major incident, a new regulation, an acquisition, a significant system change or a new threat. Each review re-scores risks, checks that responses were actually implemented, and closes or re-opens entries.

> **Real world:** A register that is filled in once for an audit and never reviewed is the most common finding against risk programs. Put the review date and owner in every row, and report overdue reviews.

## Risk treatment

The objectives list four treatments. IR 8286 (Table 3) uses the same four: accept, transfer, mitigate and avoid. SP 800-39 lists five responses (acceptance, avoidance, mitigation, sharing, transfer), separating **sharing** (shifting a portion of the responsibility or liability) from **transfer** (shifting all of it, with insurance as its example), and notes that self-initiated transfer such as buying insurance is generally not possible for public-sector bodies whose liability is set by law.

| Treatment | What it means | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Mitigating** (reducing) | Apply controls that lower the likelihood or the impact to an acceptable level | MFA, patching, segmentation, backups, training |
| **Transferring** (sharing) | Shift some of the consequences to another party | **Cyber insurance**; contract clauses that make a vendor liable; outsourcing card processing to a payment provider |
| **Avoiding** | Stop or never start the activity that creates the risk | Retire an unsupported application; decide not to store card numbers at all; do not enter a market whose laws you cannot meet |
| **Accepting** | Take no further action beyond monitoring because the risk is within tolerance (or the cost of treatment exceeds the benefit) | Formally sign off the risk of a low-value kiosk; document a time-limited **exception** for a legacy system |

Points the exam likes:

- **Insurance is transfer.** IR 8286: transfer reduces risk "by sharing a portion of the consequences with another party (e.g., cybersecurity insurance)", and "there are often consequences that cannot be transferred, like loss of customer trust". SP 800-39 adds that transfer "reduces neither the likelihood of harmful events occurring nor the consequences". **Accountability is never transferred.**
- **Avoid vs mitigate:** if the activity continues with new controls, it is mitigation. If the activity stops, it is avoidance. Replacing the vulnerable system with a different one is often described as mitigation; switching off the service entirely is avoidance.
- **Acceptance must be explicit:** an owner with the authority signs it, for a stated period, with a reason, and it is recorded in the register. Ignoring a risk is not acceptance. An **exception** (or exemption) is acceptance of non-compliance with a specific policy or standard, usually time-limited with compensating controls.
- Treatments combine: mitigate the ransomware risk with EDR and backups, then transfer part of the residual financial impact with insurance, then accept what remains.

## Business-level considerations

### Business impact analysis vs risk assessment

The NIST glossary (from SP 800-34 Rev. 1) defines a **business impact analysis (BIA)** as the "process of analyzing operational functions and the effect that a disruption might have on them". A BIA asks: which business functions matter most, what happens to them over time if they stop, and how quickly they must come back (MTD, RTO, RPO; Domain 3.4). It does not care **why** the function stops. A **risk assessment** asks which threats could cause harm, how likely they are and how bad the result would be. The BIA's impact ratings feed the risk assessment's impact values; the risk assessment's results feed the choice of controls.

| | BIA | Risk assessment |
|---|---|---|
| Starting point | Business functions and their dependencies | Threats, vulnerabilities and assets |
| Main output | Criticality, MTD, RTO, RPO, recovery priorities | Likelihood, impact, risk level, treatment options |
| Feeds | Continuity and DR plans | Risk register and control selection |

### Risk appetite, tolerance and threshold

| Term | Definition | Who sets it | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Risk appetite** | "The types and amount of risk, on a broad level, [an organization] is willing to accept in its pursuit of value" (NIST glossary, from COSO via IR 8286) | Senior leadership (enterprise level) | "Email service shall be available during the large majority of a 24-hour period" (IR 8286's own example) |
| **Risk tolerance** | "The organization or stakeholders' readiness to bear the remaining risk after responding to or considering the risk" (IR 8286); OMB A-123: "the acceptable level of variance in performance relative to the achievement of objectives" | Usually set at the program, business unit or system level, within the appetite | "Email outages must not exceed 4 hours in any 24-hour period" |
| **Risk threshold** | Not named in the SY0-801 objectives; commonly used for the specific measurable trigger at which a risk must be escalated or re-treated | Risk owner or risk function | "Any risk scoring 15 or more goes to the risk committee" |

Appetite is broad and strategic; tolerance is the measurable band around it; a threshold is the line that triggers action. Organizations describe appetite as averse, minimal, cautious, open or seeking (wording varies).

### Inherent and residual risk

- **Inherent risk:** "the risk to an entity in the absence of any direct or focused actions by management to alter its severity" (NIST glossary, from COSO).
- **Residual risk:** "risk that remains after risk responses have been documented and performed" (IR 8286); "portion of risk remaining after security measures have been applied" (SP 800-30). It is never zero.
- **Target residual risk:** the level the organization wants to reach; **actual** residual risk "should be equal to or less than the target" (IR 8286, quoting COSO). If residual risk is still above tolerance after treatment, treat it again (more controls, transfer, or avoid the activity).

### Stakeholders, oversight, regulation and law

- **Stakeholder involvement:** business owners, IT, legal, privacy, finance and HR all contribute to scoring and choosing treatments; risk decisions made by IT alone usually miss business impact.
- **Management oversight:** senior management and the board set the appetite, approve major acceptances and review the enterprise risk profile. NIST CSF 2.0's **Govern** function covers this: GV.RM-02 says "risk appetite and risk tolerance statements are established, communicated, and maintained", and GV.OV covers oversight of the risk strategy.
- **Regulatory:** some risks cannot be accepted because a regulation requires a control (PCI DSS for card data, sector rules for banks and hospitals). Regulators may also require risk assessments themselves.
- **Legal:** contracts, liability, breach notification laws and litigation exposure change the impact of a risk and can rule out acceptance. CSF GV.OC-03: "legal, regulatory, and contractual requirements regarding cybersecurity ... are understood and managed".

> **Exam tip:** "The board decided the company will not pursue any initiative that could expose customer payment data" is a statement of **risk appetite**. "Systems may be unavailable no more than 2 hours a month" is **tolerance**. The risk left after controls are applied is **residual**; before any controls it is **inherent**.

## Try it yourself

Paper exercise. For each situation, name the treatment and say what remains:

1. Contoso buys a cyber policy covering ransom negotiation and business interruption up to $2 million.
2. Contoso shuts down an old public FTP server that partners stopped using last year.
3. Contoso puts a WAF in front of a vulnerable application until the vendor ships a fix.
4. The CFO signs a 6-month exception for a legacy payroll system that cannot run EDR, with extra log review.

Answers: 1 transfer (the breach still happens; reputation and accountability stay with Contoso). 2 avoid (the risk from that server is gone). 3 mitigate (residual risk from WAF bypasses). 4 accept, documented as an exception with compensating controls (the unmonitored-endpoint risk remains until it expires).

## Key takeaways

- The risk register records each risk's description, category, score, owner, current controls, response, residual risk and status; it is the main communication tool and must be reviewed regularly and after major events.
- Four treatments: mitigate (controls), transfer (insurance, contracts), avoid (stop the activity), accept (formally, within tolerance). NIST also names sharing.
- Insurance transfers financial consequences, not accountability or reputational harm.
- BIA analyzes the effect of disruption on business functions (MTD/RTO/RPO); a risk assessment analyzes threats, likelihood and impact.
- Appetite (broad, set by leadership), then tolerance (measurable variance within it), then threshold (the trigger for action). Inherent risk is before controls, residual after.
- Management oversight, stakeholders, regulation and law constrain which treatments are acceptable.
