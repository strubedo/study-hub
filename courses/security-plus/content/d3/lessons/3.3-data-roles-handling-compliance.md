## People, places and rules

The previous lesson covered what data is and how to transform it. This one covers who is responsible for it, how it is handled where it lives and travels, how it moves through its life cycle, and which kinds of data carry legal or contractual obligations.

## Data protection roles

| Role | Responsible for | Typical person |
|---|---|---|
| **Data owner** | Accountable for a data set: decides its classification, who may access it, and acceptable use; approves exceptions | A senior business manager (VP of Sales owns the CRM data) |
| **Data steward** | Day-to-day governance of the data's meaning and quality: definitions, metadata, data quality rules, checking that handling follows policy and that classification criteria still fit | A business analyst or data governance lead |
| **Data custodian** | Implements and operates the technical protections the owner requires: storage, access control configuration, encryption, backups, restores | Database, storage or system administrators |
| **Data operator** | Uses and runs the systems that process the data day to day under the owner's rules: running jobs, entering and updating records | Operations staff, clerks, application operators |
| **Data controller** | (GDPR) The person or body that, alone or jointly, **determines the purposes and means** of processing personal data | The company that collects customers' data |
| **Data processor** | (GDPR) Processes personal data **on behalf of** the controller | A payroll or cloud CRM provider |
| **Data subprocessor** | A processor engaged **by another processor** for specific processing on the controller's behalf | The email delivery service the payroll provider uses to send payslips |

Notes on sources and wording:

- NIST's glossary (CNSSI 4009) describes **data stewards** as enforcing policies, assessing classification criteria and testing compliance; the owner/steward/custodian split above is the common governance model.
- "Data operator" is not defined in NIST or the GDPR; the description above is the reading that fits CompTIA's list (the people who operate the processing, distinct from the custodian who secures the platform).
- The GDPR does not use the word "subprocessor". Article 28(2) says a processor shall not engage **another processor** without the controller's prior specific or general written authorization (with notice of changes so the controller can object), and Article 28(4) requires the same data protection obligations to be imposed on that other processor by contract. Industry contracts call it a subprocessor. The original processor remains liable to the controller for the subprocessor's performance.
- The **data subject** is the identified or identifiable natural person the personal data relates to (GDPR Article 4(1)).

> **Exam tip:** Owner decides, custodian implements, steward governs quality and policy compliance. Controller decides why and how personal data is processed; processor and subprocessor act on instructions.

## Data handling

### Endpoints

Laptops, phones and removable media are where data leaves controlled systems. Endpoint handling controls: full-disk encryption, endpoint DLP agents (block copying restricted files to USB or personal cloud), device control, mobile device management with remote wipe, screen privacy, and no local copies of restricted data unless approved.

### Marking and labeling

**Labeling** attaches the classification to the data itself, so systems and people can apply the right handling: metadata labels that DLP and email gateways read (sensitivity labels in Microsoft Purview, for example), headers and footers, watermarks, and physical labels on media and paper. **Marking** is the visible indication on the document or medium; government schemes specify exact markings (EO 13526 requires classified information to be marked with its level, the classifying authority and declassification instructions). Unlabeled data tends to be handled at the lowest level.

### Geofencing

Geofencing applies policy based on location: conditional access that allows the HR application only from the country of operation, mobile device management that wipes or locks a device when it leaves an area, or blocking cloud console logins from unexpected countries. IP-based location can be spoofed through VPNs and proxies, so geofencing complements identity and device checks rather than replacing them.

### Data location and data placement

**Data location** is where data physically resides, including every copy: primary storage, replicas, backups, caches, logs and SaaS sub-processors. It determines sovereignty and residency obligations (Domain 3.1). **Data placement** is the deliberate decision about which systems, zones, regions or tiers hold which data: restricted data only in the restricted zone and the approved region, no production PII in development or test environments (use masked or synthetic data), and archives in cheaper, immutable storage. You cannot protect or delete data whose location you do not know, so data discovery and an up-to-date data inventory come first.

## Data management life cycle

| Stage | What happens | Security focus |
|---|---|---|
| **Creation** | Data is created, collected or received | Classify and label at creation; collect only what is needed (data minimization); record the owner |
| **Management** | Stored, used, updated, backed up | Access control per classification, encryption at rest, integrity controls, backups, monitoring |
| **Distribution** | Shared internally or externally, transmitted, published | Encrypted channels, DLP, approved sharing tools, contracts with recipients, labels that travel with the data |
| **Retention** | Kept for a defined period | Retention schedule driven by law, regulation and business need; legal holds suspend deletion; keep it no longer than needed |
| **Disposal** | Destroyed when retention ends | Sanitize per NIST SP 800-88 Rev. 2: **clear**, **purge** or **destroy**; document with a certificate of sanitization |

On disposal, SP 800-88 Rev. 2 (September 2025) explains that multi-pass overwriting gives very little assurance on flash media such as SSDs with overprovisioning; use a **purge** technique (the device's standardized sanitize commands, including **cryptographic erase**) or **destroy** the media. For cloud storage, where you cannot touch the physical media, cryptographic erase may be the only viable purge option. Degaussing does not work on SSDs.

## Data compliance

Different kinds of data carry different obligations. Know what kind of data each category covers; Domain 5 covers the governance side.

| Category | What it covers | Examples of governing rules |
|---|---|---|
| **Standards** | Industry or contractual frameworks an organization adopts or must follow | PCI DSS (payment cards), ISO/IEC 27001, NIST frameworks |
| **Health data** | Information about a person's physical or mental health and care | HIPAA protected health information (PHI) in the U.S.; GDPR "data concerning health" (a special category) |
| **Personal information** | Any information relating to an identified or identifiable person (PII; GDPR "personal data") | GDPR, national and state privacy laws |
| **Financial** | Payment card data, bank account and financial records | PCI DSS (cardholder data: at minimum the full primary account number, PAN); financial privacy and reporting laws |
| **Child/minor data** | Personal data of children | U.S. COPPA (children under 13, verifiable parental consent for online collection); GDPR Article 8 (parental consent for online services below 16, which member states may lower to no less than 13) |
| **Intellectual property** | Trade secrets, designs, source code, patents, copyrighted material | Trade secret and copyright law; NDAs; protected mainly by classification, DLP and access control |
| **Legal data** | Attorney-client privileged communications, litigation material, contracts, records under legal hold | Court rules on discovery and legal holds; privilege must be protected from disclosure |

PCI DSS also defines **sensitive authentication data** (card verification codes, full track data, PINs and PIN blocks), which must not be stored after authorization.

## Try it yourself

Paper exercise: write a one-page data inventory for an imaginary small online shop. For each data set (customer accounts, orders, card payments, support emails, employee records, product designs) record: owner, custodian, classification, location (system and country), compliance category, retention period and disposal method. Then circle every data set whose location you would have to ask a SaaS provider about.

## Key takeaways

- Owner decides and is accountable; steward governs meaning, quality and policy compliance; custodian implements protections; operator runs the processing.
- GDPR: controller determines purposes and means; processor acts on its behalf; a subprocessor is "another processor" engaged with the controller's authorization under the same obligations.
- Handling: secure endpoints, label data so systems can enforce policy, geofence where location matters, and know every location a copy lives in.
- Life cycle: creation, management, distribution, retention, disposal. Classify at creation; sanitize at disposal (clear, purge, destroy; crypto erase for SSDs and cloud).
- Compliance categories: standards, health, personal, financial, child/minor, intellectual property and legal data each bring their own obligations.
