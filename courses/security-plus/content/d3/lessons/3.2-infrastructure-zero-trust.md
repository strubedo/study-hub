## From principles to placement

Domain 1 introduced Zero Trust principles and defense in depth. Objective 3.2 is a "given a scenario, manage" objective: you will be shown a network and asked where a device goes, which zone a system belongs in, what a failure should do, or which Zero Trust component made a decision. This lesson covers infrastructure considerations, failure modes and Zero Trust architecture; the next covers secure communication and identity.

## Security zones

A **security zone** is a group of systems with the same trust level and the same policy requirements, separated from other zones by an enforcement point. FortiOS and PAN-OS make this literal: interfaces are assigned to zones and policy is written between zones. Typical zones:

| Zone | Contents | Policy idea |
|---|---|---|
| **Untrusted (internet)** | Everything outside | Deny inbound by default |
| **Screened subnet (DMZ)** | Public-facing services: reverse proxies, web front ends, mail relays, public DNS | Reachable from the internet on specific ports only; may initiate only the specific internal connections it needs |
| **Internal / user** | Workstations, printers, internal apps | No inbound from the internet; outbound through web filtering |
| **Restricted / data** | Databases, file servers with confidential data, payment systems | Reachable only from named application servers on named ports |
| **Management** | Jump hosts, management interfaces of firewalls, switches, hypervisors, out-of-band consoles | Reachable only from admin workstations or a jump host, with MFA |
| **Guest / IoT / OT** | Untrusted or special-purpose devices | Internet only (guest), or tightly limited to their controllers |

The rule of thumb: **put a system in the least trusted zone that still lets it do its job, and let traffic cross zones only through a policy**. A web server that must be reachable from the internet belongs in the DMZ, not on the internal network; the database behind it belongs in a restricted zone, not in the DMZ.

## Device placement

Where you place a device determines what it can see and stop.

- **Firewalls** sit at zone boundaries: the internet edge, between the DMZ and internal zones, and in front of restricted zones. An internal segmentation firewall catches lateral movement that the edge never sees.
- **IPS** must be **inline** (traffic passes through it) to block. An **IDS** can sit on a SPAN port or network TAP and only detects. Place the sensor where the traffic you care about is decrypted and visible; behind a TLS-terminating proxy, for example.
- **WAF and reverse proxy** in front of web servers, usually in the DMZ.
- **Load balancers** in front of the server pool they distribute to; they often terminate TLS, so they are also an inspection point.
- **VPN concentrators and ZTNA gateways** at the edge, terminating into a zone whose policy controls what remote users reach.
- **Jump hosts / bastions** in the management zone, as the only path into management interfaces.
- **Sensors and collectors** (NetFlow, log collectors) where they can see the flows of interest and reach the SIEM.

## Attack surface

The attack surface is the sum of points where an attacker can try to enter or extract data: open ports, exposed services, APIs, interfaces, accounts and people. Architecture reduces it by exposing only what must be exposed (close or filter everything else), removing unused services and protocols, putting management interfaces on a separate management network, consolidating internet exposure through a few hardened entry points (reverse proxy, VPN or ZTNA gateway), and removing default and unused accounts. Every new feature, integration or zone link adds surface, so review it during change management.

## Diversity

Diversity means using different technologies, vendors or controls at different layers so that one vulnerability or misconfiguration does not defeat all of them. An outer firewall from one vendor and an inner firewall from another means a single firmware flaw cannot bypass both. The trade-off is real: two rule languages, two management consoles, two patch streams and more skills to maintain, and complexity itself causes errors. Diversity is a defense-in-depth choice to make deliberately where the risk justifies it. (Platform diversity for resilience is covered in Domain 3.4.)

## Failure modes

What should a security device do when it fails: crashes, loses power, runs out of resources or loses its license?

| Term | Meaning | Favors |
|---|---|---|
| **Fail-open** | Traffic or access is allowed when the control fails | Availability |
| **Fail-closed** | Traffic or access is blocked when the control fails | Confidentiality and integrity |
| **Fail-safe** (RFC 4949, via the NIST glossary) | A failure mode that prevents damage to specified resources, including **life** (but the failure might still cause a security compromise) | Safety |
| **Fail-secure** (RFC 4949) | A failure mode that prevents **loss of secure state** (but might cause damage to some resource) | Security |

Applying them:

- **Perimeter or segmentation firewall: fail closed.** It is the enforcement point; if it passed everything when its policy engine failed, the boundary would vanish exactly when nobody is watching. Availability is handled with an HA pair, not by failing open.
- **Inline IPS: a deliberate choice.** Many inline IPS deployments use bypass hardware or software bypass so that traffic keeps flowing (fail open) if the inspection engine fails, because a firewall behind it still enforces policy and an outage of a critical link is unacceptable. Where confidentiality outweighs availability, configure it to fail closed. Exam stems state the priority; follow it.
- **Electrified door locks: depends on the door.** In lock hardware, a **fail-safe** lock unlocks when power is lost (typical for doors on an exit path, so people are never trapped) and a **fail-secure** lock stays locked when power is lost (typical for server rooms and cash rooms, usually with free mechanical egress from the inside). When life safety and security conflict, life safety wins; building and fire codes require it.

> **Watch out:** "Fail-safe" is about preventing harm, not about keeping things secure. A fail-safe door is *less* secure during a power cut, on purpose. Domain 2 covered the software version of failing open: an exception handler that lets a request through.

## Zero Trust architecture (NIST SP 800-207)

Domain 1 covered the seven tenets. Architecturally, SP 800-207 separates a **control plane** (where decisions are made and paths are set up) from a **data plane** (where application traffic flows), with these core components:

- **Policy engine (PE):** makes and logs the decision to grant, deny or revoke access to a resource for a subject, using enterprise policy and external inputs fed into a **trust algorithm**.
- **Policy administrator (PA):** executes the PE's decision by establishing or shutting down the communication path, through commands to the PEP; it can generate session-specific authentication tokens or credentials.
- Together, the PE and PA form the **policy decision point (PDP)**.
- **Policy enforcement point (PEP):** enables, monitors and terminates connections between the subject and the resource. It can be split into a client-side agent and a resource-side gateway, or be a single portal.

Inputs to the PE named in SP 800-207 include a **continuous diagnostics and mitigation (CDM)** system (asset state: patched OS, approved software, known vulnerabilities), an industry compliance system, threat intelligence feeds, network and system activity logs, data access policies, the enterprise PKI, the ID management system and the SIEM.

The three bullets in the SY0-801 objectives map onto these inputs:

### User authentication

Every request is authenticated, not just the first login of the day. Strong authentication (phishing-resistant MFA), attributes from the ID management system (role, department), and context (time, location, behavior) feed the trust algorithm. Re-authentication can be triggered when risk changes, though SP 800-207 notes that constantly prompting the subject is a usability cost to balance.

### Device management: health and inventory

- **Inventory:** the enterprise must know which devices exist and which are enterprise-owned, BYOD or unknown. An unknown device is not automatically trusted just because valid credentials are typed on it; policy may deny it or give it only limited access.
- **Health (posture):** OS patch level, EDR running, disk encryption on, no prohibited software, certificate present. CDM and endpoint management systems report it; an unhealthy device can be denied or sent to remediation even when the user's credentials are valid.

### Application access control

Access is granted **per application (resource) and per session**, not to a network. Instead of a VPN that puts the user on a subnet, a ZTNA gateway (a PEP) publishes only the specific application the policy allows. The user never sees other systems, which shrinks the attack surface and blocks lateral movement.

SP 800-207 also describes deployment variations: device agent/gateway, enclave-based (a gateway in front of a group of resources), resource portal (a single gateway without an agent) and application sandboxing.

### A worked request

alice opens hr.example.com from ws01. The ZTNA gateway (PEP) forwards the request to the PA and PE. The PE checks: MFA passed, alice's role allows HR self-service, but CDM reports EDR stopped on ws01. The trust algorithm returns **deny** (or limited access, by policy), the PE logs it, and the PA tells the PEP to refuse the session. When EDR is restarted and posture is reported healthy, the next request is allowed and the PA configures the PEP to permit that session only.

## Try it yourself

Paper exercise: list ten devices or systems on your home or work network (router, NAS, smart TV, laptops, a game console, a printer, a camera). Assign each to a zone from the table above and draw the allowed flows between zones. Then decide, for your router's firewall and for your front door's lock, whether each should fail open or fail closed, and why.

## Key takeaways

- Zones group systems of equal trust; traffic crosses zones only through policy. Public services go in the screened subnet, data in restricted zones, management interfaces in a management zone.
- An IPS must be inline to block; an IDS on a SPAN or TAP only detects.
- Reduce attack surface by exposing only what is needed and isolating management.
- Diversity of vendors and controls prevents one flaw from defeating every layer, at the cost of complexity.
- Firewalls fail closed; an inline IPS may fail open when availability is the stated priority; exit doors fail safe (unlock) for life safety, secure rooms fail secure.
- SP 800-207: PE decides, PA executes, together the PDP; the PEP enforces. CDM, threat intel, logs, PKI, ID management and SIEM feed the PE.
- Zero Trust access depends on user authentication, device health and inventory, and per-application access control.
