## Choosing a model is a trade-off

No architecture wins on every axis. A public SaaS service may give better availability than your own data center but less control over where data lives; an air-gapped network gives strong isolation but is slow to patch and recover. Objective 3.1 lists the axes to compare: eight **technical** and seven **business** considerations. In scenario questions, one stated constraint usually decides the answer, so learn what each consideration means and which model it tends to favor.

## Technical considerations

### Availability

The proportion of time a system is usable when needed, often stated as "nines" (99.9% allows about 8.8 hours of downtime a year; 99.99% about 53 minutes). Public cloud providers offer multiple availability zones and regions that would be expensive to build yourself, but the customer must actually **design** for them (deploy across zones, replicate data). A single-zone cloud deployment is no more available than one data center. On-premises availability depends on your own redundancy (Domain 3.4).

### Resilience

The ability to **withstand and recover** from disruption, including attacks, not just to stay up on a good day. Microservices and autoscaled stateless workloads degrade gracefully when one part fails; a monolith on one server does not. Resilience also covers dependencies: a SaaS outage, a lost interconnect in a hybrid design, or a single identity provider that everything relies on.

### Proprietary versus open source

| | Proprietary | Open source |
|---|---|---|
| Code review | Only the vendor (and auditors under NDA) can inspect it | Anyone can inspect it; popularity does not guarantee anyone has |
| Patching | Vendor schedule; you cannot fix it yourself | You or the community can patch; you must track upstream fixes |
| Support | Contracted support, SLAs, liability terms | Community or paid third-party support |
| Lock-in | Formats, APIs and licensing can lock you in | Easier to move, fork or self-host |
| Risk examples | End-of-life products with no fixes; vendor-only firmware | Abandoned projects, malicious packages, unpatched forks |

Neither is inherently more secure. The question is whether you can verify and maintain what you run.

### Usability

Security that people cannot use gets bypassed. A remote-access design that requires three separate logins encourages shared accounts and shadow IT; a well-integrated SSO with phishing-resistant MFA is both more usable and more secure. Usability also applies to administrators: a design so complex that nobody understands the rule base produces misconfigurations.

### Responsibility

Who is accountable for each control: the shared responsibility split in cloud (previous lesson), the contract with a managed service provider, and internal ownership between network, server and application teams. Gaps appear where each party assumes the other handles something, such as logging in SaaS, backups of SaaS data, or patching of a vendor-managed appliance. Write the split down (a responsibility matrix) for every model you use.

### Compute

How much processing a model provides and how it is shared. Cloud compute is elastic and pay-per-use; on-premises compute is fixed by what you bought. Multitenant compute means sharing hardware with other customers (isolation relies on the hypervisor); dedicated hosts cost more. Edge and OT devices often have so little compute that agents, encryption or inspection cannot run on them, which pushes controls to the network.

### Power requirements

Every on-premises or edge design must supply and back up power (UPS, generators, redundant feeds, Domain 3.4) and cooling to match. High-density compute such as GPU racks can exceed a room's electrical and cooling capacity. Moving to cloud transfers the power problem to the provider; remote sites and OT may still need their own.

### Ease of recovery

How quickly and reliably you can rebuild after failure or compromise. IaC and immutable images let you redeploy a clean environment in minutes; stateless services can simply be replaced; a hand-built server with years of undocumented changes cannot. Ease of recovery drives the realistic RTO (Domain 3.4), and it is a strong argument for automation in every model.

## Business considerations

### Data sovereignty

The principle that data is subject to the **laws of the jurisdiction** that governs it, typically the country where it is stored or processed and, for some laws, the country where the provider is incorporated. Related but distinct terms:

- **Data residency** is *where* data physically resides, often chosen by policy or contract (for example, "EU region only").
- **Data localization** is a legal requirement that certain data stay inside a country.

Example: the GDPR (Chapter V, Article 44) permits transfers of personal data to third countries only if the chapter's conditions are met. A cloud region choice therefore becomes a compliance decision, and multicloud or hybrid designs must keep regulated data in permitted locations.

### Data classification

The classification of the data (Domain 3.3) limits which models are acceptable. Public marketing content can live anywhere; restricted data may be confined to a private cloud, a specific region or an air-gapped enclave, and some contracts or regulations prohibit multitenant hosting.

### Cost

Capital expenditure (buying hardware and facilities) versus operating expenditure (subscriptions, usage-based billing). Cloud lowers entry cost but can grow quickly with data egress, logging and storage; on-premises costs are front-loaded and include staff, power and refresh cycles. Security controls cost money in every model, and an unbudgeted control is usually the first to be dropped.

### Ownership

Who owns the infrastructure, the data and the intellectual property, and therefore who can make decisions about it. In SaaS you own your data but not the platform; check contract terms on data export, deletion at termination and access by the provider. On-premises you own everything, including every problem.

### Environmental requirements

The physical conditions a model needs or must survive: temperature and humidity, dust and vibration on a factory floor, flood and earthquake risk at a site, and sustainability targets. OT and edge equipment often needs ruggedized hardware; a data center needs HVAC and fire suppression. Choosing a cloud region in a different geography can also reduce exposure to a regional disaster.

### Scalability

How easily capacity grows (or shrinks) with demand. Public cloud and serverless scale automatically; on-premises scaling means procurement lead times. Security tooling must scale too: a firewall or licence sized for today's throughput becomes a bottleneck or a fail-open risk tomorrow.

### Risk

The overall business risk of the model: likelihood and impact of compromise, outage, vendor failure, lock-in and non-compliance, compared with the organization's risk appetite (Domain 5). Every other consideration feeds this one. Outsourcing transfers some operational risk to a provider but not accountability for the data.

## Comparing the models side by side

| | On-premises | Public cloud (IaaS/PaaS) | SaaS | Air-gapped |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Control | Full | Shared | Least | Full |
| Responsibility on you | All layers | Workload, data, identity, configuration | Data, accounts, tenant settings | All layers |
| Availability potential | What you build | High, if designed across zones and regions | Provider SLA | Limited by manual processes |
| Ease of recovery | Depends on automation | High with IaC and snapshots | Provider-dependent; back up your own data | Slow; manual transfer |
| Scalability | Slow (procurement) | Fast | Provider-managed | Very slow |
| Cost profile | Capex, staff | Opex, usage-based | Subscription | High per system |
| Sovereignty | Known location | Choose region; check provider jurisdiction | Often limited choice | Known location |

## Worked example

Contoso wants to move its customer portal. Requirements: (1) customer PII must remain in the EU, (2) the portal must survive the loss of one data center, (3) the team has two engineers. A single on-premises data center fails requirement 2. A private cloud across two Contoso-owned sites meets 1 and 2 but is hard for two engineers to run. A public cloud PaaS deployment in two availability zones of an EU region meets all three, provided Contoso configures it to stay in that region, writes it as IaC, and documents what it still owns: data, identities, application security and logging.

## Key takeaways

- Technical considerations: availability, resilience, proprietary vs open source, usability, responsibility, compute, power requirements, ease of recovery.
- Business considerations: data sovereignty, data classification, cost, ownership, environmental requirements, scalability, risk.
- Cloud availability is potential, not automatic: you must design across zones and regions.
- Data sovereignty is about which laws apply; data residency is where data sits; localization is a legal requirement to keep it in-country.
- Responsibility gaps (logging, SaaS backups, vendor-managed patching) are where breaches hide; write the split down.
- Ease of recovery comes from automation and statelessness; it sets the realistic RTO.
- In scenario questions, find the hard constraint; it eliminates all but one model.
