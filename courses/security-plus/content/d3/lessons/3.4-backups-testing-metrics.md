## Storage and backups

**Storage** resilience (RAID, replicated arrays, snapshots, cloud object storage with multiple copies) keeps data available when a disk or node fails. **Backups** let you go back in time after deletion, corruption, ransomware or a site loss. Storage redundancy cannot do that: RAID and replication faithfully copy the corruption. Snapshots on the same array are useful for quick rollbacks but are lost with the array and are often deleted by ransomware operators with administrative access.

### Backup types and restore chains

SP 800-34 Rev. 1 describes three methods:

| Type | What it copies | Backup time and size | What a restore needs |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Full** | Everything in scope | Longest, largest | The one full backup |
| **Incremental** | Changes since the **last backup of any type** | Shortest, smallest | The last full **plus every incremental since**, applied in order |
| **Differential** | Changes since the **last full** backup | Grows each day until the next full | The last full **plus only the latest differential** |

Worked example. Full backups run Sunday at 01:00; a daily job runs at 01:00 Monday to Saturday. The server fails on Thursday at 15:00.

- With **incrementals**: restore Sunday's full, then Monday's, Tuesday's, Wednesday's and Thursday's incrementals, in that order. If Tuesday's media is unreadable, everything after Monday is lost.
- With **differentials**: restore Sunday's full, then Thursday's differential.
- Either way, changes made between 01:00 and 15:00 on Thursday are **lost (14 hours)**, unless transaction logs or replication cover them.

Snapshots and synthetic full backups (built by merging incrementals on the backup server) are variations that shorten restore time.

### Retention

How long each backup is kept, and how many versions. Retention must satisfy legal and regulatory minimums (and data-minimization maximums), and it must be **longer than an attacker's likely dwell time**: if ransomware sat dormant for 40 days and you keep 30 days of backups, every copy contains it. A common scheme is grandfather-father-son (daily, weekly and monthly sets kept for increasing periods).

### Immutability

An **immutable** backup cannot be changed or deleted until its retention period expires, even by an administrator: write-once-read-many (WORM) media, object lock on cloud storage, or hardened backup repositories. NIST SP 800-82 Rev. 3 describes immutable storage as backup storage that adds integrity through a read-only format. Immutability, plus an **offline** or logically air-gapped copy, defeats the attacker who compromises the backup console and deletes every restore point.

The **3-2-1 rule** (CISA): keep **3** copies of important data (the primary and two backups), on **2** different media types, with **1** copy offsite. Many organizations extend it to require one copy immutable or offline.

### Scope

What a backup includes: just data, or the whole system (OS, applications, configuration)? Application-consistent (databases quiesced) or crash-consistent? Does it include configuration of network devices (FortiGate and PAN-OS config backups), identity systems (Active Directory, the identity provider), infrastructure as code, encryption keys and certificates, and **SaaS data** (under shared responsibility, the provider keeps the service running, but restoring a user's deleted mailbox from last year may be your job)? Missing scope is discovered during a disaster.

### Restoration testing

A backup that has never been restored is a hope, not a control. Test restores regularly to an isolated environment: verify files open and databases mount, time the restore (that is your real RTO), confirm that keys and credentials needed for the restore are available when the primary systems are down, and scan restored data for malware before returning it to production.

## Testing the plans

SP 800-34 Rev. 1 (in its supplemental guidance for control CP-4) gives examples of test methods: checklist, walk-through/tabletop, simulation, parallel and full interrupt, which run roughly from least to most disruptive. It names these methods but does not define them, so the descriptions below are the common BC/DR meanings. The SY0-801 objectives name three:

| Test | What happens | Disruption and risk |
|---|---|---|
| **Simulation** | A realistic scenario is staged and teams carry out their recovery roles against it (for example, a simulated ransomware outage in a test environment), without failing over production | Low; tests people and procedures |
| **Parallel processing** | The alternate site or system is brought up and processes real or copied workloads **at the same time** as the primary; outputs are compared. Production keeps running on the primary | Medium; proves the alternate can do the work without risking production |
| **Failover** | Production is actually switched to the redundant system or alternate site (and later failed back) | Highest; the only test that proves the switch works end to end; schedule it in a maintenance window with a backout plan |

Tabletop exercises (discussion only) come before all three, and SP 800-34 relates test depth to system impact: a tabletop for low-impact systems, a functional exercise for moderate, and a full-scale functional exercise (which includes a system failover to the alternate site) for high-impact systems.

## Disaster recovery, business continuity and capacity planning

- **Business continuity (BC)** keeps the organization's essential functions operating during and after a disruption, including people, facilities, suppliers and manual workarounds. The **business impact analysis (BIA)** identifies critical processes and their tolerable downtime.
- **Disaster recovery (DR)** is the IT part: restoring systems, data and infrastructure after a disaster, following the DR plan (who declares a disaster, where systems recover, in which order).
- **Capacity planning** makes sure resources match demand, both normally and in a crisis: enough people (cross-trained staff, not one expert), technology (an alternate site that can carry the full load, licences that allow failover), and infrastructure (bandwidth, power, storage for restores). A DR site sized for 50% of production fails during a real disaster at 100% load.

## Recovery metrics

| Metric | Meaning | Measured from |
|---|---|---|
| **RTO** (recovery time objective) | Maximum time a system can be unavailable before the impact is unacceptable (SP 800-34) | Business requirement, from the BIA |
| **RPO** (recovery point objective) | The point in time to which data must be recoverable: the maximum tolerable **data loss**, measured backwards from the disruption | Business requirement |
| **MTTR** (mean time to repair) | Average time to repair a failed component and restore it to service | Operational history: total repair time / number of repairs |
| **MTBF** (mean time between failures) | Average operating time between failures of a repairable component | Operational history: total operating time / number of failures |

SP 800-34 also defines **maximum tolerable downtime (MTD)**, the total outage the business can accept; the RTO must be shorter than the MTD to leave time for work such as reprocessing data. RTO and RPO are **targets**; MTTR and MTBF are **measurements**.

### Worked numbers

**RPO from a schedule.** Backups every 6 hours (00:00, 06:00, 12:00, 18:00). A failure at 17:45 loses everything since 12:00: **5 hours 45 minutes**. The worst case for this schedule is just under 6 hours, so it meets an RPO of 6 hours but not an RPO of 4 hours, which would need backups at least every 4 hours (or replication).

**RTO against MTD.** The BIA gives an MTD of 8 hours. Restoring the database takes 3 hours, rebuilding the application servers 2 hours, and reprocessing the lost transactions 2 hours. Because the 2 hours of reprocessing must also fit inside the MTD, the system's RTO can be at most 8 − 2 = **6 hours**. The measured restore (3 + 2 = 5 hours) meets it, and the whole outage (7 hours) stays within the MTD.

**MTBF and MTTR.** Three identical firewalls each ran a full year (8,760 hours): 26,280 operating hours with 6 failures. MTBF = 26,280 / 6 = **4,380 hours**. The six repairs took 30 hours in total: MTTR = 30 / 6 = **5 hours**.

**Availability.**

> Availability = MTBF / (MTBF + MTTR)

For one firewall: 4,380 / (4,380 + 5) = 4,380 / 4,385 = **0.99886, about 99.89%**. Halving the MTTR to 2.5 hours (spares on site) gives 4,380 / 4,382.5 = 99.94%. Lowering MTTR is often cheaper than raising MTBF. For a pair in an HA cluster, the service is down only if both are down at once, which is why clustering raises availability far above one unit's.

## Try it yourself

Paper exercise: (1) Your backup job runs nightly at 02:00 with a full backup on Saturday and incrementals on other days. A disk fails Wednesday at 11:30. Write the restore chain and the data loss. (Saturday full plus Sunday, Monday, Tuesday and Wednesday incrementals; 9 hours 30 minutes lost.) (2) A switch has an MTBF of 50,000 hours and an MTTR of 4 hours. Compute its availability (50,000 / 50,004 = 99.992%). (3) On any computer, check when your own important files were last backed up, and restore one file to a different folder to prove it works.

## Key takeaways

- Redundancy (RAID, replication, snapshots on the same system) is not backup.
- Full = everything; incremental = since the last backup of any type (restore all in order); differential = since the last full (restore full plus latest).
- Retention longer than dwell time, immutable and offline copies, and the 3-2-1 rule defeat ransomware that targets backups.
- Define scope (configs, identity, keys, SaaS data) and test restores; the timed restore is your real RTO.
- Tests: simulation (staged scenario), parallel processing (alternate runs alongside primary), failover (production actually switches).
- RTO = max downtime; RPO = max data loss; both are targets. MTTR and MTBF are measured; availability = MTBF / (MTBF + MTTR).
