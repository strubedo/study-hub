## Resilience is a security property

Availability is one third of the CIA triad, and ransomware has made recovery a core security skill: an organization that can restore cleanly and fail over quickly takes away an attacker's leverage. Objective 3.4 covers how architecture keeps services running (sites, diversity, redundancy, power) and how you prove you can recover (backups, testing, metrics, next lesson).

## Alternate site considerations

NIST SP 800-34 Rev. 1 (contingency planning) describes three common alternate site types, from least to most ready:

| Site | What is there (SP 800-34) | Recovery time | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cold** | Space and basic infrastructure: power, HVAC, environmental controls; **no** equipment or telecommunications installed | Longest: equipment must be acquired, installed and loaded with software and data; days to weeks | Lowest (mainly the lease) |
| **Warm** | Cold-site infrastructure **plus** computer and telecommunications equipment, but **not** loaded with current software or data | Hours to days: revert to production software levels, load data from backup, connect users | Middle |
| **Hot** | Fully operational equipment, current production software, data loaded and kept up to date, often synchronized concurrently with the primary | Shortest: minutes to hours | Highest: near-duplicate operations and staff |

SP 800-34 also describes **mobile sites** (self-contained transportable shells fitted with equipment) and **mirrored sites** (fully redundant, with automated real-time mirroring; the most expensive, near 100% availability). A cloud region can act as any of these: pilot-light infrastructure as code that is deployed only after a disaster is cold or warm; an always-running replica in a second region is hot.

**Choose by RTO and cost**: the cheapest site whose realistic recovery time still meets the required RTO. A site that must also serve as a security boundary needs the same controls as production; an alternate site with weaker firewall rules or no logging is an attractive target during a crisis.

### Environmental considerations

- **Geographic separation:** far enough away that the same flood, hurricane, wildfire, earthquake or regional power failure does not hit both sites, close enough for staff to reach it and for replication latency to be acceptable (synchronous replication needs short distances).
- **Site risk:** flood plains, fault lines, flight paths, industrial hazards, local utility reliability.
- **Facility conditions:** HVAC capacity, fire suppression, physical security equal to the primary.
- **Legal environment:** an alternate site or region in another country brings its data sovereignty rules with it (Domain 3.1).

## Platform diversity

Using different platforms so that one defect, vulnerability or supply problem cannot take everything down:

- **Vendor platform:** different vendors for redundant components, for example primary and secondary internet providers on different physical paths, or firewalls from two vendors in series. A bug that crashes one vendor's OS will not crash the other.
- **Hardware:** different models or manufacturing batches, so a firmware flaw or a bad batch of disks does not fail every unit at once.
- **Virtualization:** the ability to run workloads on more than one hypervisor or cloud, or to recover virtual machines to different hardware; virtualization itself decouples workloads from specific hardware, which makes recovery to dissimilar equipment possible.

Diversity trades simplicity for resilience: more skills, more patching streams, harder automation. Apply it to the components whose common-mode failure would hurt most.

## Redundancy strategies and solutions

| Solution | What it does | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Load balancing** | Distributes requests across several servers (active-active) and stops sending to members that fail health checks | Also scales capacity; session persistence and TLS termination are design choices; the load balancer itself needs a redundant pair |
| **Clustering** | Several nodes work as one system, often with shared or replicated storage and a quorum; a standby node takes over when the active one fails (active-passive), or all nodes serve (active-active) | Firewall HA pairs (FortiGate FGCP, PAN-OS HA) are clusters that synchronize sessions and configuration |
| **Autoscaling** | Automatically adds or removes instances based on load or schedule | Absorbs spikes and some DDoS, but raises cost; instances must be stateless and built from hardened images |
| **High availability (HA)** | The design goal of minimizing downtime by removing single points of failure (redundant components, paths and sites) with automatic failover | Measured as availability percentage; every layer counts, including DNS, identity and power |
| **Multicloud systems** | Running a service across more than one cloud provider so a provider-wide outage does not stop it | Highest independence from one provider, at the cost of different IAM, tooling and data synchronization |

Redundancy is not backup: a cluster replicates a deleted table or encrypted files to every node instantly.

### Availability arithmetic

| Availability | Downtime per year (365.25 days) |
|---|---|
| 99% | about 87.7 hours |
| 99.9% | about 8.77 hours |
| 99.99% | about 52.6 minutes |
| 99.999% ("five nines") | about 5.26 minutes |

## Power

| Component | Role | Limits |
|---|---|---|
| **Uninterruptible power supply (UPS)** | Batteries supply power instantly when utility power fails; many models also condition power (sags, spikes, noise) | Runtime of minutes; long enough to ride through short outages, let the generator start, or shut down gracefully |
| **Redundant power supply (RPS)** | A device with two (or more) power supplies, each able to carry the load, ideally fed from separate circuits (A and B feeds, separate PDUs) | Protects against a failed supply or a tripped circuit; not against a building-wide outage |
| **Power generator** | Fuel-powered generation for long outages, connected through a transfer switch | Takes seconds to start and stabilize, so a UPS bridges the gap; needs fuel contracts and regular load testing |
| **Surge protector** | Diverts or clamps voltage spikes (lightning, switching surges) to protect equipment | Provides no power during an outage |

The chain for a data center is typically: utility feed, transfer switch to the generator, UPS, PDUs (A and B), dual power supplies in each device. Each element covers a different failure.

## Putting it together: a resilient web service

Contoso's order portal runs in two availability zones behind a pair of load balancers, with autoscaled stateless web servers built from IaC, a clustered database with synchronous replication between the zones, and asynchronous replication to a warm environment in a second region. On-premises, the firewalls are an HA cluster with dual power supplies on A and B feeds, a UPS and a generator. Two internet providers enter the building by different physical paths. The remaining single points of failure, written down and accepted or fixed: the single identity provider, and the one person who knows the DNS registrar login.

## Try it yourself

Paper exercise: list every component between your laptop and a website you use daily (laptop, Wi-Fi access point, router, modem, ISP, DNS resolver, the site's CDN, the site). Mark each single point of failure and write the redundancy solution that would remove it, and whether it would need UPS, RPS or generator power. Then compute the yearly downtime allowed by 99.95% availability (answer: about 4.38 hours).

## Key takeaways

- Cold: space and power only; warm: equipment but no current data or software; hot: running and synchronized. Choose the cheapest site that meets the RTO.
- Separate sites geographically enough to avoid shared disasters, and secure the alternate site like production.
- Platform diversity (vendor, hardware, virtualization) prevents common-mode failure at the cost of complexity.
- Load balancing distributes, clustering fails over, autoscaling adds capacity, HA removes single points of failure, multicloud removes dependence on one provider.
- UPS bridges seconds to minutes and conditions power; generators run for hours or days; RPS protects against a failed supply or circuit; surge protectors only clamp spikes.
- Redundancy replicates mistakes and ransomware; it does not replace backups.
