## Frameworks and the objectives' order

The SY0-801 objectives list incident response activities in this order:

1. **Preparation**
2. **Identification**
3. **Investigation**
4. **Containment**
5. **Negotiation**
6. **Eradication/recovery**
7. **Notification/external reporting**
8. **Post-incident**

Learn this order for the exam. In practice the activities overlap and loop: investigation continues during containment, notification deadlines can start running during identification, and a new finding sends you back to containment.

**How it maps to NIST.** The classic NIST SP 800-61 Rev. 2 life cycle had four phases: preparation; detection and analysis; containment, eradication and recovery; post-incident activity. **SP 800-61 Rev. 3** (April 2025) replaced that model with a **CSF 2.0 Community Profile**. Its Table 1 maps the old phases to CSF 2.0 Functions: preparation to **Govern, Identify and Protect**; detection and analysis to **Detect**; containment, eradication and recovery to **Respond** and **Recover**; and post-incident activity to the **Improvement** category of **Identify** (ID.IM), which the table also attaches to detection and to response, because lessons come from every phase. Rev. 3 stresses that preparation activities are broader cybersecurity risk management, not only incident response, and that lessons learned feed improvement continuously rather than only at the end. The objectives' "identification" and "investigation" together correspond to detection and analysis; NIST places incident reporting and notification within Detect, Respond and Recover; it does not name "negotiation" as an activity, so placing it under Respond is this course's mapping.

## Preparation

Everything done before an incident so that response is fast and correct:

- **Roles:** an incident response plan naming the incident commander/lead, analysts, forensics, IT operations, communications/PR, legal, HR, management and external parties (MSSP, retained IR firm, cyber insurer). Contact lists with out-of-band methods (if email is compromised, how do you reach people?).
- **Playbooks:** step-by-step procedures for common incident types: phishing, ransomware, account compromise, data exposure, DDoS. Runbooks hold the technical steps.
- **Training:** for responders (tools, forensics, playbooks) and for staff (how to recognize and report).
- **Testing:**
  - **Tabletop exercises:** discussion-based; participants talk through a scenario ("the file servers are encrypted and a ransom note demands payment") to test decisions, roles and communication. No systems are touched.
  - **Simulation:** a more realistic, often hands-on exercise: a simulated attack or injected events that responders must detect and handle with real tools, possibly against a test environment or with a red team.
- **Tools and access in place before you need them:** logging and retention, EDR, forensic workstations and write blockers, clean jump kits, backups tested for restore, pre-approved authority to isolate systems.

## Identification

Recognizing that an incident is happening and scoping it:

- **Detection:** SIEM alerts, EDR detections, IDS alerts, DLP, user reports, help desk tickets, anomalies in dashboards. Triage confirms whether an event is an incident, assigns severity and opens a case.
- **Internal advisory:** information from inside the organization: a security team bulletin, a report from another department, an internal threat intelligence notice ("we are seeing phishing that impersonates payroll").
- **External advisory:** information from outside: vendor security advisories, CISA alerts and the KEV catalog, ISAC bulletins, law enforcement notifications, a researcher's report, a customer noticing their data for sale.
- **Threat hunting:** analysts proactively search for threats that evaded automated detection, starting from a **hypothesis** (for example "an attacker is using scheduled tasks for persistence on servers") and searching logs and telemetry to prove or disprove it. Hunting assumes compromise rather than waiting for an alert.

## Investigation

Determining what happened, how, when, what was affected and who is responsible:

- **Digital forensics:** collecting and analyzing evidence in a way that preserves its integrity (lesson 4.8): memory and disk images, logs, network captures, following the **order of volatility** (most volatile first).
- **Chain of custody:** NIST's definition is a process that tracks the movement of evidence through its collection, safeguarding and analysis by documenting each person who handled it, the date and time it was collected or transferred, and the purpose. A gap (an undocumented transfer, an unsealed bag) can make evidence inadmissible.
- **E-discovery:** the process of identifying, preserving, collecting, reviewing and producing electronically stored information (email, documents, chat, logs) for litigation or regulatory requests. Incident data may later become subject to e-discovery.
- **Preservation:** preventing the destruction or alteration of relevant data: image systems before rebuilding, export logs before they roll over, suspend automatic deletion, and apply a **legal hold** when litigation is reasonably anticipated (Domain 5, 5.4).

## Containment

Stopping the spread and the damage while preserving evidence:

- **Quarantine/isolation:** network-isolate an infected host through EDR (it can still talk to the EDR console), move it to an isolated VLAN, disable a switch port, block attacker IPs and domains at the firewall and DNS, disable compromised accounts and revoke their sessions and tokens, take a compromised server out of the load balancer pool.
- Prefer isolation over pulling the power: powering off destroys memory evidence (running processes, network connections, encryption keys).
- **Short-term** containment buys time; **long-term** containment keeps business running safely (for example rebuilt clean servers in an isolated segment) while eradication is prepared.

## Negotiation

Some incidents involve communication with the attacker or with other parties, most often in **ransomware and extortion** cases. Negotiation decisions are made by management with **legal counsel**, often a specialist negotiation firm and the cyber insurer, and usually with **law enforcement** informed. Considerations: whether payment is legal (sanctions rules may prohibit paying certain groups), whether it is necessary (are backups intact?), proof that the attacker actually has the data or a working decryptor, buying time for recovery, and the fact that payment does not guarantee deletion of stolen data. Never let responders negotiate informally. Negotiation can also mean coordinating with vendors, partners, insurers or regulators about responsibility and terms.

## Eradication and recovery

- **Eradication** removes the cause: delete malware and persistence (scheduled tasks, services, registry run keys, web shells), close the exploited vulnerability, remove attacker-created accounts, reset compromised credentials (including service accounts and, in an AD compromise, the `krbtgt` account twice), and often **rebuild** systems from known-good images rather than trying to clean them.
- **Recovery** restores normal operation: restore data from clean backups (verify they predate the compromise), bring systems back in stages, monitor closely for reinfection, and confirm with the business owners that systems work.

## Notification and external reporting

Who must be told, and by when:

- **Stakeholders:** executives, the board, business owners, affected internal teams, IT, insurers, partners under contract.
- **Customers:** when their data or service is affected, per contracts and law; clear, accurate, coordinated with legal and communications.
- **Law enforcement:** for criminal activity (national police or cybercrime units); they may help with attribution, decryptors or recovering funds, and may ask for a delay in public notification.
- **Mandatory reporting:** obligations set by law, regulation or contract, with deadlines. Example: the GDPR (Article 33) requires notifying the supervisory authority of a personal data breach without undue delay and, where feasible, not later than **72 hours** after becoming aware of it, unless the breach is unlikely to result in a risk to individuals. Sector rules (health, finance, critical infrastructure, public companies) set their own deadlines. Legal counsel decides what applies.

## Post-incident

- **Lessons learned:** a blameless meeting soon after the incident with everyone involved: what happened, what worked, what did not, what would we change? Feed results into plans, playbooks, controls and training.
- **Root cause analysis (RCA):** find the underlying cause, not just the symptom: not "a user clicked a link" but "legacy authentication without MFA allowed a phished password to be used". Techniques include the "5 whys" and timeline analysis.
- **Post-incident reporting (PIR):** a written report with the timeline, scope, impact, actions taken, root cause, and recommendations with owners and deadlines. It serves management, auditors, insurers and, if needed, regulators.

## Try it yourself

Paper exercise. Run a 30-minute tabletop with yourself or friends: "At 08:15 the help desk receives three calls that shared drives show files ending in `.locked` and a text file asking for payment." For each of the eight activities, write what you would do, who you would call and what evidence you would keep. Then put these actions in order: isolate the file server; check that last night's backups are clean; image the file server; call legal and the insurer; restore from backup; hold the lessons-learned meeting; notify the regulator if personal data was taken.

## Key takeaways

- Objectives' order: preparation, identification, investigation, containment, negotiation, eradication/recovery, notification/external reporting, post-incident.
- SP 800-61 Rev. 3 (2025) maps the old four phases to CSF 2.0: preparation to Govern/Identify/Protect, detection to Detect, response to Respond/Recover, lessons to Identify (Improvement).
- Preparation: roles, playbooks, training, testing (tabletop = discussion, simulation = hands-on).
- Identification: detection, internal and external advisories, hypothesis-driven threat hunting.
- Investigation: forensics, chain of custody, e-discovery, preservation and legal hold.
- Contain by isolating, not powering off; negotiate only with legal and management; eradicate causes and rebuild; notify within mandatory deadlines (GDPR: 72 hours to the authority); finish with lessons learned, RCA and a PIR.
