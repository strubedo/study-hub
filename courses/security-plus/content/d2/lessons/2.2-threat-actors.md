## Why actors matter

Defenses are chosen against someone. A volunteer hacktivist group with off-the-shelf DDoS tools and a foreign intelligence service with custom implants call for different priorities, different detection and different incident handling. NIST SP 800-150 describes threat actors as ranging "from individual, autonomous attackers to well-resourced groups operating in a coordinated manner as part of a criminal enterprise or on behalf of a nation-state". SP 800-30 characterizes adversarial threat sources by **capability, intent and targeting**, which is what the exam's three **attributes** capture.

## The three attributes

| Attribute | Question it answers | Range |
|---|---|---|
| **Internal / external** | Does the actor start with legitimate access? | Insiders and accidental users are internal; most others are external. External actors often try to *become* internal by stealing credentials or recruiting an insider |
| **Resources / funding** | How much money, staff, time and infrastructure? | From one person with free tools to a state agency with a budget |
| **Level of sophistication / capability** | How advanced are their skills and tools? | From running public exploit scripts to developing zero-days and custom implants |

Two more ideas are not separate bullets but are implied: **intent** (what they want, the motivation) and **persistence** (how long they will keep trying).

## The eight threat actors

**Crime syndicate / organized crime.** Profit-driven groups organized like businesses, with specialists for initial access, malware development, money laundering and negotiation. Ransomware gangs, business email compromise rings and carding operations are typical. External, well funded (by their own crimes), moderately to highly sophisticated. Motivations: **financial**, **extortion**.

**Terrorist.** Groups that use violence or the threat of it to further political, religious or ideological goals. In cyber terms they seek **fear/chaos** and disruption, and may target critical infrastructure or use the internet for propaganda, recruitment and financing. Capability varies widely; some have little technical skill. Motivations: **ideological**, **political**, **fear/chaos**, **influence**.

**Unskilled attacker.** Someone who uses tools and exploits written by others without understanding them (the older term "script kiddie"). External, few resources, low sophistication, but able to cause real damage with automated tools against unpatched systems. Motivations: **notoriety**, **general curiosity**, sometimes **revenge** or petty profit.

**Hacktivist.** Hacks to promote a cause: defacing websites, leaking documents, DDoS against organizations they oppose. Usually external, loosely organized and volunteer-funded, sophistication low to moderate. Motivations: **ideological**, **political**, **ethical** (in their own view), **influence**, **notoriety** for the cause.

**Insider.** A current or former employee, contractor or partner who **deliberately** misuses legitimate access. NIST defines insider threat as an insider using authorized access, "wittingly or unwittingly", to do harm; the exam splits off the unwitting case as a separate actor. Internal by definition, resources limited to their own, but knowledge of the environment and existing access make them dangerous and hard to detect. Motivations: **revenge** (a disgruntled employee), **financial** (selling data, paid by a competitor or criminal group), **espionage**, **ideological**.

**Accidental / unintentional.** An authorized user who causes harm by mistake: mailing a spreadsheet to the wrong recipient, making a storage bucket public, clicking a phishing link, misconfiguring a firewall rule. Internal, no malicious intent, so no "motivation" applies. Training, guardrails, least privilege and good change management are the main controls.

**Competitor.** A rival business that seeks an unfair advantage: stealing designs, customer lists or bid prices, or disrupting a rival's service at a critical time. Often works through insiders or hired contractors. Resources can be substantial. Motivations: **intellectual property**, **espionage** (commercial), **financial**.

**State-sponsored (nation-state).** Government agencies or groups they direct or fund. Highest resources and sophistication, long-term objectives, patient and stealthy. They are the actors most often behind **advanced persistent threats (APTs)** (lesson 2.3). Motivations: **espionage** (government, military, commercial), **political**, **influence** (disinformation, election interference), **intellectual property**, and in conflict, disruption (fear/chaos). Some state groups also raise money through crime (**financial**).

## The twelve motivations

| Motivation | What the actor wants | Typical actors |
|---|---|---|
| **Financial** | Money: theft, fraud, sale of data or access, cryptomining | Organized crime, insiders, some state groups |
| **Influence** | Shape opinion, policy or decisions | State-sponsored, hacktivist, terrorist |
| **Intellectual property** | Designs, source code, formulas, research | Competitor, state-sponsored, insider |
| **Notoriety** | Fame, reputation, bragging rights | Unskilled attacker, hacktivist |
| **Espionage** | Secret information on governments, militaries or companies | State-sponsored, competitor, insider |
| **Fear / chaos** | Disruption, panic, loss of confidence | Terrorist, state-sponsored (in conflict) |
| **Extortion** | Payment in exchange for not doing harm: decryption keys, not leaking data, stopping a DDoS | Organized crime |
| **General curiosity** | To see whether it can be done, or what is there | Unskilled attacker |
| **Revenge** | Payback for a perceived wrong such as being fired | Insider (often former), unskilled attacker |
| **Ideological** | Advance a belief system or cause | Hacktivist, terrorist |
| **Political** | Change or protest a government, policy or election | Hacktivist, state-sponsored, terrorist |
| **Ethical** | Act on a moral conviction, for example exposing wrongdoing or perceived unsafe practices | Hacktivist, sometimes an insider whistleblower |

> **Exam tip:** Read the scenario for the actor's *goal*, then the *attributes*. "Demands payment or the stolen files go on a leak site" is extortion by organized crime. "Copies of the product roadmap appeared at a rival's launch" is a competitor seeking intellectual property. "Defaced the homepage with a protest message" is a hacktivist. "Years of quiet access, custom malware, targeting defense research" is state-sponsored espionage.

> **Watch out:** Financial and extortion overlap. Extortion is the specific mechanism (pay us or we harm you); financial is the broader goal. When a question describes a ransom demand, extortion is the more precise motivation. Ideological and political also overlap: political is about governments, elections and policy; ideological is about a wider belief system.

> **Real world:** Attribution is hard. Criminal groups buy tools state groups once used, state groups sometimes pose as hacktivists, and insiders are often recruited by outsiders. Analysts attribute with stated confidence levels, using TTPs as well as indicators.

## Actors at a glance

| Actor | Internal/external | Resources/funding | Sophistication | Main motivations |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Organized crime | External | High | Medium to high | Financial, extortion |
| Terrorist | External | Low to medium | Low to medium | Ideological, political, fear/chaos |
| Unskilled attacker | External | Low | Low | Notoriety, curiosity, revenge |
| Hacktivist | External (sometimes insiders) | Low to medium | Low to medium | Ideological, political, ethical, notoriety |
| Insider | Internal | Low (own) but has access | Low to high | Revenge, financial, espionage |
| Accidental | Internal | n/a | n/a | None (no intent) |
| Competitor | External (often via insiders) | Medium to high | Medium | Intellectual property, espionage, financial |
| State-sponsored | External | Very high | Very high | Espionage, political, influence, IP |

## Try it yourself

Paper exercise. For each headline, name the actor, the most precise motivation and the three attributes: (1) "Former database administrator deletes production tables the day after dismissal." (2) "Group posts a protest video on a city's website and leaks council emails." (3) "Teenager uses a downloaded scanner to break into thousands of home cameras and posts screenshots." (4) "Payroll file sent to an outside address because of email autocomplete." (5) "Ransomware encrypts a hospital's servers; the group threatens to publish patient data unless paid." Answers: insider/revenge; hacktivist/political or ideological; unskilled attacker/notoriety; accidental/none; organized crime/extortion.

## Key takeaways

- Eight actors: organized crime, terrorist, unskilled attacker, hacktivist, insider, accidental/unintentional, competitor, state-sponsored.
- Three attributes: internal/external, resources/funding, sophistication/capability (SP 800-30's capability, intent and targeting).
- Twelve motivations: financial, influence, intellectual property, notoriety, espionage, fear/chaos, extortion, general curiosity, revenge, ideological, political, ethical.
- Insider = deliberate misuse of legitimate access; accidental = harm without intent.
- State-sponsored actors are the most capable and are the usual source of APTs; organized crime is the usual source of ransomware and extortion.
- Pick the most precise motivation: extortion over financial for a ransom demand, intellectual property over espionage when the target is a design or source code.
