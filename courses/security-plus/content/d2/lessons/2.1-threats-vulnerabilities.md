## Threats, vulnerabilities and risk

Three words get mixed up constantly, and the exam relies on keeping them apart:

- A **threat** is any circumstance or event that could harm the organization through unauthorized access, destruction, disclosure, modification of information or denial of service (NIST, from FIPS 200). The person or group behind a deliberate threat is a **threat actor** (lesson 2.2).
- A **vulnerability** is a weakness that a threat can exploit: an unpatched service, a hardcoded password, a door that does not latch.
- **Risk** combines the two: how **likely** it is that a threat exploits a vulnerability, and how bad the **impact** would be. Domain 5 covers risk analysis in depth; here you need the two inputs.

## Likelihood and impact

NIST SP 800-30 Rev. 1 defines **likelihood of occurrence** as a weighted factor based on the probability that a given threat is capable of exploiting a given vulnerability. It is assessed in three steps: how likely the threat event is to be **initiated** (or, for accidents and natural events, to occur), how likely it is to **result in adverse impact** once it happens, and the overall likelihood combining the two. For adversaries, likelihood rests on their **intent, capability and targeting**; for non-adversarial threats (a flood, a disk failure, an admin error) it rests on historical and empirical data. Likelihood is always for a time frame ("in the next 12 months").

**Impact** is the effect on operations, assets, individuals or other organizations of a loss of confidentiality, integrity or availability (NIST, from FIPS 199). Impact depends on the asset: the same ransomware hitting a test VM and the payroll database has the same likelihood but very different impact.

> **Exam tip:** Likelihood is about the threat and the exposure (is exploit code public, is the host on the internet, is the actor targeting us?). Impact is about the asset (what happens to the business if it is lost?). A question that asks you to raise the priority because "the server holds regulated customer data" is talking about impact.

## Threat intelligence: sources and feeds

NIST SP 800-150 separates **threat information** (any information about a threat that helps you protect yourself or detect an actor) from **threat intelligence** (threat information that has been aggregated, analyzed, interpreted or enriched to support decisions). Its major types of threat information are:

| Type | What it is | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Indicators** | Technical artifacts or observables suggesting an attack is imminent, underway or has happened | C2 server IP address, malicious domain, URL, file hash, phishing subject line |
| **TTPs** | Tactics, techniques and procedures: how an actor behaves | "Gains access with password spraying, then uses PowerShell to dump credentials" |
| **Security alerts** | Short technical notices (advisories, bulletins, vulnerability notes) | A CISA advisory, a vendor PSIRT bulletin |
| **Threat intelligence reports** | Prose analysis of actors, targets and TTPs | A quarterly report on a ransomware group |
| **Tool configurations** | Recommended settings for security tools | A detection rule or an IPS signature |

**Intelligence sources** come in several kinds:

- **Open-source intelligence (OSINT):** public websites, researcher blogs, social media, public vulnerability databases, free community feeds.
- **Commercial / proprietary:** paid feeds and reports from security vendors, often bundled with a product.
- **Government:** national CERTs and agencies. CISA's **Automated Indicator Sharing (AIS)** is a free service that exchanges indicators over **STIX/TAXII** (CISA says to use a TAXII 2.1 client).
- **Information sharing communities:** sector **ISACs** (Information Sharing and Analysis Centers), ISAOs, CERTs/CSIRTs, law enforcement and peer organizations.
- **Internal:** your own SIEM, firewall, EDR, IDS and incident records. SP 800-150 tells organizations to identify these first; they are the most relevant source you have.
- **Dark web monitoring:** criminal forums and leak sites, usually watched by commercial services, can reveal stolen credentials or access for sale.

A **threat feed** is a continuously updated, usually machine-readable stream of threat information, mostly indicators, that tools ingest automatically: a SIEM matches feed IPs and domains against logs, a firewall blocks a feed's address list, an EDR blocks a feed's hashes. **STIX** is the common format for describing threat information and **TAXII** is the protocol for exchanging it.

**Handling labels.** Shared intelligence carries a **Traffic Light Protocol (TLP)** label. FIRST's TLP version 2.0 (authoritative from August 2022) has four labels: **TLP:RED** (named recipients only), **TLP:AMBER** (need-to-know within the recipient's organization and its clients; **TLP:AMBER+STRICT** limits it to the organization only), **TLP:GREEN** (within the recipient's community) and **TLP:CLEAR** (no limit; this replaced TLP:WHITE).

> **Watch out:** Indicators go stale quickly. Attackers change IPs and domains in hours, and a shared hosting IP can be malicious today and a legitimate customer's tomorrow. TTPs change slowly, which is why behavior-based detection outlasts IP blocklists. Feeds need an expiry policy, and blocking on a feed needs care.

## The threat intelligence life cycle

The objectives list "life cycle" under threats. The model most programs use, and the one this course teaches, is the threat intelligence life cycle; it is a widely used industry model rather than a NIST-defined one:

1. **Planning and direction (requirements):** decide what decision-makers need to know. "Which actors target payment processors, and how do they get in?"
2. **Collection:** gather data from the sources above.
3. **Processing:** normalize, de-duplicate, translate and enrich the raw data (for example, map indicators to STIX objects).
4. **Analysis:** turn information into intelligence: assess relevance, confidence and what it means for this organization.
5. **Dissemination:** deliver it in the right form: indicators to the SIEM, a summary to executives, a detection rule to the SOC.
6. **Feedback:** consumers say what was useful, which refines the next round of requirements.

The *attacker's* life cycle (reconnaissance through actions on objectives, as in the Cyber Kill Chain and MITRE ATT&CK tactics) is taught with the frameworks in Domain 5. Threat intelligence describes where in that life cycle an indicator or TTP is seen.

## Vulnerabilities: CVE, CWE and CVSS

Three acronyms, three different jobs:

| | **CVE** | **CWE** | **CVSS** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Answers | *Which* vulnerability? | *What kind* of weakness? | *How severe* is it? |
| Is | An identifier for one publicly known vulnerability | A category in a list of software and hardware weakness types | A 0.0-10.0 severity score plus a vector string |
| Example | CVE-YYYY-NNNNN (year, then a 4-19 digit number) | CWE-367 Time-of-check Time-of-use race condition | `CVSS:3.1/AV:N/AC:L/PR:N/UI:N/S:U/C:H/I:H/A:H` = 9.8 |
| Run by | CVE Program (MITRE operates it; CISA sponsors) | MITRE CWE Program | FIRST |

**How CVE works.** A **CVE ID** has the form `CVE-YYYY-NNNN`: the year, then a sequence number of 4 to 19 digits (the CVE record schema's own rule). The year shows when the ID was reserved or published, not when the bug was introduced. IDs are assigned by **CVE Numbering Authorities (CNAs)**: vendors, open-source projects, coordination centers, bug bounty providers and research groups, each for its own scope. A CVE record moves through three states: **Reserved** (ID assigned, details not yet public), **Published** (description, affected products and versions, and references added) and **Rejected** (the ID should not be used, but stays on the list so people know it is invalid). The **NVD** (run by NIST) is synchronized with the CVE List and adds analysis such as CVSS scores, CWE mapping and affected-product data.

**Vulnerability types.** Records are classified by weakness type (CWE). The broad types you meet in this domain are software flaws (injection, memory corruption, race conditions, broken authentication), misconfigurations, weak or broken cryptography, hardware and firmware flaws, and process weaknesses such as unpatched or unsupported systems. Lesson 2.4 goes through each attack surface.

### CVSS: what the score means

CVSS is an open framework from FIRST for communicating the characteristics and **severity** of vulnerabilities. Both the v3.1 and v4.0 documents state that CVSS measures **severity, not risk**: the Base score assumes a reasonable worst case across all environments and knows nothing about your asset, your exposure or your controls. Severity bands are the same in both versions:

| Rating | None | Low | Medium | High | Critical |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Score | 0.0 | 0.1-3.9 | 4.0-6.9 | 7.0-8.9 | 9.0-10.0 |

| Metric group | CVSS v3.1 | CVSS v4.0 | Who supplies it |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Base** (intrinsic, constant) | AV, AC, PR, UI, **S** (Scope), C, I, A | AV, AC, **AT** (Attack Requirements), PR, UI, **VC/VI/VA** (vulnerable system) and **SC/SI/SA** (subsequent systems) | Vendor or analyst |
| Changes over time | **Temporal**: Exploit Code Maturity (E), Remediation Level (RL), Report Confidence (RC) | **Threat**: Exploit Maturity (E) only: Attacked, POC, Unreported | Consumer, from threat intelligence |
| Your environment | **Environmental**: CR/IR/AR security requirements plus modified base metrics | **Environmental**: same idea | Consumer |
| Extra context, no score effect | none | **Supplemental**: Safety, Automatable, Provider Urgency, Recovery, Value Density, Vulnerability Response Effort | Either |

Base metric values to know: **AV** Network / Adjacent / Local / Physical; **AC** Low / High; **PR** None / Low / High; **UI** None / Required in v3.1, None / Passive / Active in v4.0; impacts High / Low / None. In v4.0, Scope was replaced by separate vulnerable-system and subsequent-system impacts, and the Temporal group was renamed **Threat** (Remediation Level and Report Confidence were retired). If Exploit Maturity is not defined, v4.0 assumes the worst case, **Attacked**.

**Vector strings** start with the version and list metric:value pairs separated by slashes, for example `CVSS:4.0/AV:N/AC:L/AT:N/PR:N/UI:N/VC:H/VI:H/VA:H/SC:N/SI:N/SA:N`. v4.0 adds nomenclature that says which groups went into a number: **CVSS-B** (Base), **CVSS-BT** (Base + Threat), **CVSS-BE** (Base + Environmental) and **CVSS-BTE** (all three).

## Prioritization

You cannot patch everything at once, so prioritize using risk, not the Base score alone:

1. **Known exploitation:** is it in CISA's **Known Exploited Vulnerabilities (KEV)** catalog? A KEV entry requires an assigned CVE ID, reliable evidence of active exploitation and a clear remediation action. CISA recommends prioritizing KEV entries.
2. **Exploit probability:** FIRST's **EPSS** estimates the probability (0 to 1) that a CVE will be exploited in the wild in the next 30 days, published daily.
3. **Severity:** the CVSS score, ideally with Threat and Environmental metrics applied (CVSS-BTE).
4. **Exposure and asset criticality:** internet-facing versus isolated, and what the asset holds or does (impact).
5. **Compensating controls:** a WAF rule or segmentation can lower likelihood while the patch is scheduled.

> **Exam tip:** A Medium CVSS vulnerability that is in KEV on an internet-facing server usually outranks a Critical one on an isolated lab host. When a question says "most urgent", look for active exploitation plus exposure plus asset value. Vulnerability management tasks (scanning, remediation, reporting) are Domain 4.

## Try it yourself

Use any browser on any OS. Open FIRST's official CVSS v4.0 calculator (search "FIRST CVSS 4.0 calculator") and enter `AV:N/AC:L/AT:N/PR:N/UI:N` with High vulnerable-system impacts and no subsequent-system impact. Note the score and severity, then change **AV** to Physical and **PR** to High and watch the score fall. Then set Exploit Maturity to Unreported and see that the CVSS-BT score drops below the Base score. Paper alternative: take the vector `CVSS:3.1/AV:L/AC:L/PR:L/UI:N/S:U/C:H/I:H/A:H` and write out in words what each metric means (it scores 7.8, High).

## Key takeaways

- Threat = what could cause harm; vulnerability = the weakness; risk = likelihood × impact. Likelihood comes from the threat and exposure, impact from the asset.
- Intelligence sources: OSINT, commercial, government (CISA AIS), ISACs and other communities, internal telemetry, dark web monitoring. Feeds deliver mostly indicators; STIX describes, TAXII transports; TLP 2.0 labels RED, AMBER(+STRICT), GREEN, CLEAR.
- Threat intelligence life cycle: requirements, collection, processing, analysis, dissemination, feedback.
- CVE identifies one vulnerability (`CVE-YYYY-NNNN+`, assigned by CNAs); CWE names the weakness type; CVSS scores severity.
- CVSS measures severity, not risk. Bands: Low 0.1-3.9, Medium 4.0-6.9, High 7.0-8.9, Critical 9.0-10.0. v4.0 groups: Base, Threat, Environmental, Supplemental.
- Prioritize with KEV, EPSS, CVSS-BTE, exposure and asset criticality, not the Base score alone.
