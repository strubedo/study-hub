## Vectors and sources

A **threat vector** is the path or means an attacker uses to reach a target: an email, a QR code, a USB stick, a VPN portal. The **source** is where the threat comes from: the actor, or a compromised third party such as a supplier. The objective lists many vectors; this lesson covers the ones that rely on content and people (APT, message, image, attachment, browser and human-based). The next lesson covers network, remote access, endpoint, supply chain, media, IoT/OT, physical and signal vectors.

The exam tests two skills: recognizing the vector in a scenario, and knowing why it works, which points to the control.

## Advanced persistent threat (APT)

NIST defines an APT as an adversary with sophisticated expertise and significant resources that uses multiple attack vectors (cyber, physical, deception) to establish and extend its presence in a target's infrastructure, usually to steal information or undermine critical operations, and that pursues its objectives **repeatedly over an extended period**, adapts to defenders and stays determined to keep the access it needs.

- **Advanced:** custom tools, zero-days, careful operational security.
- **Persistent:** months or years of access; if evicted, they come back.
- **Threat:** a capable, directed adversary, most often state-sponsored (lesson 2.2), though some criminal groups operate this way.

An APT is a *source* that uses all the vectors below. The tell-tale signs in a scenario are long dwell time, low-and-slow activity, targeted reconnaissance, multiple persistence mechanisms and data staged for exfiltration.

## Message-based vectors

| Vector | How it is abused | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Email** | Phishing links, malicious attachments, business email compromise (BEC), spoofed or lookalike senders | Still the most common initial access vector. SPF, DKIM and DMARC (Domain 4) authenticate the sending domain; they do not stop lookalike domains or compromised real mailboxes |
| **Short Message Service (SMS)** | Smishing: texts with links ("your parcel is held", "your MFA expires"), callback numbers | Sender IDs can be spoofed; phones show less of the URL; texts bypass corporate email filtering |
| **Rich Communication Services (RCS)** | The GSMA successor to SMS in phone messaging apps: rich media, files, read receipts and branded business messaging | Same smishing risks with richer lures (images, buttons, files); attackers may also send through bulk messaging services. Whether a conversation is end-to-end encrypted depends on the clients |
| **Instant messaging** | WhatsApp, Signal, Telegram and similar: impersonation of colleagues or executives, malicious files and links | Often on personal devices outside corporate monitoring |
| **Collaboration tools** | Teams, Slack and similar: messages from external tenants posing as the help desk, shared files and links, compromised guest accounts, abused webhooks and integrations | Users trust messages inside the corporate platform more than email |

> **Exam tip:** The vector is named by the channel. A malicious link in a text message is SMS-based (smishing, lesson 2.5); the same link in a Teams chat is a collaboration-tool vector. A voice call is vishing.

## Image-based vectors

- **QR codes:** a QR code hides its URL until scanned, so users cannot hover over it, and it moves the victim from a protected laptop to a less protected phone. The FBI has warned of criminals placing tampered QR codes (for example stickers on parking meters) to send victims to sites that steal login and payment details. QR phishing is called **quishing**. QR codes in email images also evade text-based link scanners.
- **Embedded content:** images and other objects inside messages and documents. Remote images in email act as tracking pixels (confirming that an address is live and leaking the client's IP and device type); text rendered as an image evades keyword filters; **SVG** images are XML and can contain script or links; data can be hidden in image pixels (steganography, Domain 1).
- **CAPTCHA:** a challenge meant to tell humans from bots. Attackers abuse it two ways: phishing pages put a CAPTCHA in front of the fake login so automated URL scanners cannot reach the malicious content, and **fake CAPTCHA** pages tell the user to "verify you are human" by opening the Run dialog or a terminal and pasting a command, which installs malware. MITRE ATT&CK lists this "ClickFix" pattern under User Execution: Malicious Copy and Paste.

> **Watch out:** No real CAPTCHA ever asks you to press keyboard shortcuts or paste anything into a command prompt. That instruction is the indicator.

## Attachment-based vectors

- **Embedded macros:** VBA code in Office documents (`.docm`, `.xlsm`, legacy `.doc`/`.xls`) that runs when the user enables content. Microsoft now blocks VBA macros by default in files that carry the **Mark of the Web** (files from the internet, such as email attachments and downloads), so attackers shift to containers that do not carry it, convincing the user to unblock the file, or other formats.
- **Rich Text Format (RTF) documents:** RTF cannot hold VBA macros, which makes it look safe, but it can embed OLE objects and has been used to deliver exploits for parser and component vulnerabilities in the word processor; the user only has to open or preview the file.
- **Portable Document Format (PDF):** PDFs can contain JavaScript, embedded files, form actions and links. They are used both for exploits against PDF readers and, more often, as a "safe-looking" wrapper for a phishing link or QR code.

Common controls: attachment sandboxing ("detonation"), stripping or converting active content (content disarm and reconstruction), blocking risky file types, keeping macro blocking and Protected View enabled, and patching document readers.

## Browser-based vectors

| Vector | Risk |
|---|---|
| **Extensions** | Extensions inherit the browser's access. One with "read and change all your data on all websites" can steal cookies, passwords and page content. Malicious extensions are published directly, or a popular one is bought or its developer account is compromised and a malicious update is pushed. Control: allow-list extensions by policy |
| **JavaScript** | Runs in every page. Malicious or injected scripts deliver drive-by exploits, skim card numbers from checkout forms, redirect users or run cross-site scripting (lesson 2.5) |
| **Password managers** | A high-value target: browser-saved passwords are a standard infostealer target; autofill can be tricked into filling credentials into an injected or lookalike form; a stolen vault plus a weak master password exposes everything. Dedicated managers with strong master passwords and MFA, and passkeys, reduce the risk |
| **Cookies** | Session cookies prove you are already logged in. A stolen session cookie lets an attacker reuse the session without the password **or the MFA step**. Secure, HttpOnly and SameSite attributes (Domain 4) limit theft and misuse |
| **Session tokens** | Bearer tokens (OAuth access and refresh tokens, JWTs) work the same way for apps and APIs: whoever holds the token is the user until it expires or is revoked |

> **Exam tip:** "The user completed MFA, yet the attacker accessed the mailbox from another country without an MFA prompt" points to a stolen session cookie or token (often from an adversary-in-the-middle phishing kit or an infostealer), not a password problem. Revoking sessions, not only resetting the password, is the fix.

## Human-based vectors

- **Impersonation:** pretending to be someone trusted, such as a help desk technician, an executive, a vendor or an auditor, in person, by phone or in a message. It is the core of most social engineering (lesson 2.5).
- **Contractor:** third-party staff such as cleaners, consultants and maintenance technicians often get physical or logical access with less vetting and supervision than employees, and they may work for several clients.
- **Visitor:** guests, delivery drivers and interview candidates. Unescorted visitors can plug in devices, photograph screens, tailgate into secure areas or leave media behind. Controls: sign-in, badges, escorts, visitor network isolation.
- **Biometric:** biometric data such as fingerprints, face and voice can be captured and spoofed (lifted fingerprints, photos, AI voice cloning) and, unlike a password, cannot be changed once stolen. Biometric databases are high-value targets.
- **Watering hole:** NIST describes it as infecting websites frequently visited by the target group, so that a member of that group is compromised when visiting. The attacker does not contact the victim at all; they compromise a site the victims already trust, such as an industry association portal, a supplier's site or a local restaurant's menu page.

> **Watch out:** A watering hole is targeted at a *group* through a site they already visit. A drive-by download on a random malicious ad is not targeted. A phishing email sends the victim to the attacker; a watering hole waits for the victim to come.

## Try it yourself

Works on any OS with a desktop browser. (1) Open your browser's extensions page (`chrome://extensions`, `edge://extensions`, `about:addons` in Firefox, or Safari Settings > Extensions) and, for each extension, note what site access it has. Remove any you do not use. (2) Open the browser's developer tools (F12, or Cmd+Option+I on macOS), go to the Application/Storage tab, and look at the cookies for a site you are logged in to: note which have the Secure, HttpOnly and SameSite flags. Do not copy or share any cookie values. (3) On your phone, use a QR scanner that previews the URL before opening it.

## Key takeaways

- An APT is a well-resourced, sophisticated adversary, usually state-sponsored, that keeps long-term access and returns if evicted.
- Message vectors: email, SMS, RCS, instant messaging, collaboration tools. Name the vector by the channel.
- Image vectors: QR codes (hide the URL, move users to phones), embedded content (tracking pixels, SVG script, text-as-image), CAPTCHA (hides phishing from scanners; fake CAPTCHA tells users to paste commands).
- Attachment vectors: macros (blocked by default for Mark of the Web files), RTF (no macros but embedded objects and exploits), PDF (JavaScript, links, embedded files).
- Browser vectors: extensions, JavaScript, password managers, cookies and session tokens; stolen sessions bypass MFA.
- Human vectors: impersonation, contractors, visitors, biometrics that cannot be changed, and watering holes that wait for a targeted group.
