## Network-based vectors

- **Infrastructure devices:** routers, switches, firewalls, VPN concentrators, load balancers and wireless controllers. They sit at trust boundaries, see all traffic, usually cannot run EDR and are patched less often than servers. Exposed management interfaces (web GUI, SSH, Telnet, SNMP with default community strings), default or shared admin credentials and unpatched edge-device firmware are favorite targets. Once an attacker controls a router or firewall, they can redirect, capture or alter traffic and hide in a device most SOCs barely log.
- **Virtualized devices:** virtual firewalls, routers and switches, hypervisor virtual switches and the hypervisor management plane (vCenter-style consoles, cloud consoles). Compromise of the management plane gives control of every VM on it. Virtual appliances are often deployed from old images and forgotten. VM escape is covered in lesson 2.4.
- **Session keys:** the symmetric keys that protect an established session (TLS, IPsec, SSH, Kerberos). If an attacker obtains them, from memory of a compromised host, from a debug key log left enabled, through weak key generation or by downgrading to a weak exchange, they can decrypt captured traffic or inject into the session without ever learning a password. Forward secrecy (Domain 1) limits the damage to the one session whose key was taken.

> **Real world:** For a firewall engineer, the first questions after any edge-device advisory are: is the management interface reachable from the internet, are we on a fixed version, and do our logs leave the device, so that an attacker who controls it cannot erase them?

## Remote access vectors

| Vector | Default port | Main risks |
|---|---|---|
| **Remote desktop (RDP)** | TCP/UDP 3389 | Internet-exposed RDP is brute-forced and password-sprayed constantly; unpatched RDP services; stolen credentials bought from initial access brokers. Put it behind a VPN or gateway with MFA, enable Network Level Authentication, never expose it directly |
| **Virtual Network Computing (VNC)** | TCP 5900 (+ display number) | Uses the Remote Framebuffer (RFB) protocol; some deployments have weak or no passwords and do not encrypt traffic; common on OT HMIs and lab machines. Tunnel it over SSH or a VPN |
| **Virtual private network (VPN)** | IPsec: UDP 500 and 4500; TLS VPNs: TCP 443 | The VPN gateway is an internet-facing infrastructure device: vulnerabilities in it give direct internal access; stolen credentials without MFA give a legitimate-looking tunnel; split tunneling and over-broad access after connection widen the blast radius |

Remote support tools (commercial remote monitoring and management software) are a related vector: attackers use them because they are signed, trusted and allowed through the firewall.

## Endpoint-based vectors

- **Mobile devices and tablets:** malicious apps and sideloading, smishing, lost or stolen devices, out-of-date OS versions, personal devices (BYOD) mixing work and personal data.
- **Workstations:** where users read email, browse and run attachments, so they are where most initial compromises land.
- **Servers:** fewer users but high value; internet-facing services, admin interfaces and stored data.
- **Trusted devices:** devices the network or an identity system already trusts: domain-joined laptops, devices registered for passwordless sign-in, phones paired with a car or a laptop, a jump host. Compromising one inherits that trust, which is why Zero Trust (Domain 1) rechecks device health on each request.
- **Built-in tools and living-off-the-land tools:** utilities already present on the OS, such as PowerShell, WMI, `certutil`, `rundll32`, `mshta`, `bitsadmin`, scheduled tasks, `bash`, `curl` and `ssh`. MITRE ATT&CK describes attackers proxying execution through signed, trusted binaries to evade defenses, and using utilities such as `certutil` to download tools. **Living off the land** means using these instead of dropping new malware: no new executable for antivirus to catch, signed binaries that allow lists permit, and activity that looks like administration.

> **Exam tip:** If a scenario says "no malware file was found" but logs show PowerShell with an encoded command, `certutil` downloading from the internet or WMI creating processes, think living off the land (and fileless malware, lesson 2.5). The control is behavior-based detection (EDR), script logging and restricting who can run admin tools, not signature antivirus.

## Supply chain vectors

An attacker who cannot get into the target goes through someone the target trusts. MITRE ATT&CK distinguishes manipulating products or delivery mechanisms before the customer receives them (supply chain compromise) from abusing an existing trusted connection (trusted relationship).

| Source | Example |
|---|---|
| **Third-party providers** | A software vendor's update server or build pipeline is compromised and pushes a signed malicious update; a compromised open-source dependency; counterfeit or tampered hardware |
| **Managed service providers (MSPs)** | The MSP's remote management tools and admin accounts reach every customer; one breach of the MSP becomes many breaches |
| **Logistic providers** | Couriers, shippers and warehouses can intercept and tamper with hardware in transit; delivery-themed phishing exploits the relationship |
| **Software as a service (SaaS) providers** | A breach at the provider exposes customer data; OAuth integrations and API tokens granted to a SaaS app give it standing access to your tenant |

Controls: vendor due diligence and right-to-audit clauses (Domain 5), software bills of materials, code-signing verification, least-privilege access for providers with MFA and monitoring, and network segmentation of what providers can reach.

## External media: malicious USB

- **Malware on removable media:** files that run via autorun or trick the user into opening them; ATT&CK notes this can reach air-gapped networks.
- **Keystroke-injection devices:** a "USB drive" that is really a keyboard (HID), which types commands at machine speed as soon as it is plugged in; storage-blocking policies do not stop a keyboard.
- **Malicious cables and chargers:** cables with hidden implants, or charging stations that try to exchange data with the phone.
- **Destructive devices:** devices that discharge high voltage into the port.

Controls: block or allow-list USB device classes by policy, disable autorun, scan media at a kiosk, train users never to plug in found devices.

## IoT- and OT-based vectors

**IoT** devices such as **cameras**, **sensors** and **printers** share the same problems: default credentials, rarely patched firmware, unencrypted management protocols, long service lives and no EDR. Printers store copies of scanned and printed documents, accept raw jobs on TCP 9100 and often hold directory credentials for scan-to-email. Cameras are recruited into botnets for DDoS. Sensors and building systems (BACnet on UDP 47808, MQTT on TCP 1883) are often on flat networks next to corporate devices.

**Operational technology (OT)** is hardware and software that monitors or controls physical processes: industrial control systems (ICS), SCADA, PLCs and HMIs. Many OT protocols, such as Modbus (TCP 502), were designed without authentication; equipment runs for decades and cannot be patched or rebooted on demand; and an attack can cause physical damage or safety incidents, so availability and safety come first. Segment IoT and OT away from IT, monitor them passively and control remote access strictly.

## Physical-based vectors

- **Lock and key:** picking, bumping, shimming, copying keys from a photo, master keys that were never recovered. Mechanical locks leave no audit trail.
- **Access vestibule:** two interlocked doors where the second opens only after the first closes (older term: mantrap). It defeats tailgating if it enforces one person at a time; it fails when both doors are propped, the interlock is disabled or staff hold the door.
- **Access passes:** badges and proximity cards can be lost, borrowed, cloned (many older low-frequency proximity cards have no cryptographic protection) or faked; a pass not revoked after someone leaves is a valid credential.

## Signal-based vectors

- **Bluetooth:** NIST SP 800-121 lists **bluejacking** (sending unsolicited messages to nearby Bluetooth devices, similar to spam), **bluesnarfing** (exploiting firmware flaws in older devices to force a connection and read stored data) and **bluebugging** (using a firmware flaw to take control of the device's commands), plus eavesdropping, DoS and on-path attacks against pairing. Keep devices non-discoverable, patched and paired only with known devices.
- **Radio frequency (RF):** Wi-Fi and other radio links can be eavesdropped, jammed (a DoS), imitated with rogue or evil twin access points (lesson 2.5) or replayed; for example, capturing and replaying codes from older fixed-code remote openers.
- **Near-field communication (NFC):** a few centimetres of range limits but does not prevent attack: **skimming** (reading a tag or card without the owner's knowledge, NIST SP 800-98) and **relay** attacks that forward a card's or phone's NFC exchange to a distant reader in real time.

## Try it yourself

Paper exercise: draw your home or office network and mark every vector from this lesson that applies: the router's admin page, any device with remote access enabled, IoT devices (TV, cameras, printer), USB ports, Bluetooth devices, the front-door lock. For each, write one control. On any OS you can also look at your Bluetooth settings and confirm that the device is not discoverable when you are not pairing, and check whether your router's remote management (WAN-side admin) is disabled.

## Key takeaways

- Network vectors: infrastructure devices (edge firmware, exposed management), virtualized devices (management plane), session keys (decrypt or hijack without the password).
- Remote access: RDP 3389, VNC 5900 (RFB), VPN gateways (UDP 500/4500, TCP 443). Never expose RDP or VNC directly; require MFA on VPNs.
- Endpoints: mobile, workstations, servers, tablets, trusted devices; living-off-the-land tools mean no new malware file to detect.
- Supply chain: third-party providers, MSPs, logistic providers, SaaS providers. Trust in them is the attack path.
- Malicious USB includes keystroke-injection devices and malicious cables, not just infected files.
- IoT (cameras, sensors, printers) and OT share default credentials, weak protocols and long lifespans; segment them.
- Physical: locks, access vestibules (interlocked doors) and access passes. Signal: Bluetooth (bluejacking, bluesnarfing), RF (jamming, rogue APs, replay), NFC (skimming, relay).
