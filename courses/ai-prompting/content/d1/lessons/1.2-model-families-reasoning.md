## Vendors, families and tiers

ChatGPT, Gemini and Claude are three products from three companies, each backed by its own **model family**:

| Product | Company | Model family (as of October 2026) |
|---|---|---|
| ChatGPT | OpenAI | GPT |
| Gemini | Google | Gemini |
| Claude | Anthropic | Claude |

A **family** is a line of related models trained and released by one vendor. Within a family, vendors offer **tiers** that trade capability against speed and cost. The tier names change from generation to generation, and the apps and the APIs do not always expose the same names, so learn the *pattern* and look up the names when you need them.

The common pattern is three bands:

1. **Flagship** (largest, most capable, slowest and most expensive): hard reasoning, complex coding, long agentic work.
2. **Balanced / mid-tier**: a strong all-rounder at a lower price and latency. Often the right default for daily work.
3. **Fast / small / lite**: lowest latency and cost, suited to high-volume jobs like classification, extraction, routing and simple drafting.

Many lineups also include **specialized models** for images, speech and realtime voice, or security research, separate from the general text models.

### The lineups on the vendors' own pages, as of October 2026

These come from the vendors' model overview pages as checked in October 2026. They will be out of date within months; use them to see the pattern, not to memorize.

- **Anthropic (Claude):** the models overview lists **Claude Fable 5.1**, **Claude Opus 5.5**, **Claude Sonnet 5.5** and **Claude Haiku 5.5**. The page describes Fable as for demanding reasoning and long-horizon agentic work, Opus as the suggested starting point for most workloads, Sonnet as the best combination of speed and intelligence, and Haiku as the fastest, for high-volume latency-sensitive tasks such as classification, extraction and routing. Historically Haiku (small), Sonnet (mid) and Opus (large) is the recurring naming pattern; Fable is a newer top line.
- **OpenAI (GPT):** the models page lists **GPT-6 Astra** (the flagship for complex reasoning and coding), **GPT-6.1 Sol** (near-flagship performance at lower cost), **GPT-6.1 Terra** (fast and efficient) and **GPT-6 Luna** (the most efficient, for focused high-volume tasks), plus separate image, realtime voice and specialized models.
- **Google (Gemini):** the Gemini API models page lists **Gemini 4 Argon** as the flagship reasoning model, **Gemini 3.8 Flash** as the most intelligent Flash model, and older Flash and **Flash-Lite** models (Flash-Lite being the fastest, cheapest band), plus Live (voice), text-to-speech, image (marketed as Nano Banana) and other specialized models. The page also still lists a Gemini 3.1 Pro model.

Notice that the same three bands appear under different names: flagship (Fable/Opus, Astra, Argon), balanced (Sonnet, Sol/Terra, Flash), fast (Haiku, Luna, Flash-Lite).

> **Watch out:** Names and numbers are not comparable across vendors. "Sonnet", "Sol" and "Flash" occupy roughly similar niches, but nothing guarantees equal capability, and version numbers (5.5, 6.1, 3.8) are each vendor's own scheme. Old model names are retired on a schedule; production code should pin a specific model ID and track deprecation notices.

## App model pickers versus API model IDs

In a **consumer chat app** you usually pick a mode or a friendly model name, and the vendor decides what that maps to. In an **API** you pass an exact model ID string (for example the ID format `claude-sonnet-5-5`, as the Anthropic page shows, or `gpt-6-astra`, `gemini-4-argon` on the other two). App names and API IDs can differ, plan limits can restrict which models you see, and a vendor may route automatically between a fast and a deeper model. When a question is "which model is this chat using?", look at the app's own picker or help page, not the model's answer about itself.

## Reasoning and extended-thinking models

A **reasoning model** (Anthropic says "thinking", Google says "thinking", OpenAI says "reasoning") spends extra tokens working through a problem before it writes the final answer. Those tokens are a scratchpad: it plans, tries approaches, checks itself and revises. The result is usually better on multi-step maths, debugging, planning and ambiguous tasks, at the cost of more latency and more tokens.

What the three vendors' documentation says, as of October 2026:

- **OpenAI.** Reasoning models use hidden **reasoning tokens**, which count against the context window and are billed as output tokens. A `reasoning.effort` setting controls how much the model thinks, with values such as `none`, `low`, `medium`, `high`, `xhigh` and `max`, depending on the model (GPT-6 Astra does not accept `none`). GPT-6 models also offer a separate `standard` or `pro` mode. OpenAI's guide advises giving a clear goal, constraints and output format rather than scripting every step.
- **Google.** Gemini thinking models reason before replying; a `thinking_level` parameter controls how much, most models default to dynamic thinking, thinking tokens are billed even though only optional summaries are returned, and `max_output_tokens` caps thinking and output together.
- **Anthropic.** The current Claude models use **adaptive thinking**: the model decides whether and how much to think on each request, guided by an **effort** setting. The models overview lists thinking as "adaptive (always on)" for Fable 5.1 and Opus 5.5 and "adaptive" for Sonnet 5.5 and Haiku 5.5. The older "extended thinking" with a fixed `budget_tokens` is described as a legacy feature that the newer models reject. In casual usage, people still say "extended thinking" for the general idea.

The shared ideas are stable even though parameter names will change: a **depth knob** (effort or level), **hidden or summarized reasoning** you pay for as output, and reasoning tokens that **share the context window and output cap**.

### When to use a reasoning model

- Use it for debugging subtle problems, multi-step planning, analysing conflicting evidence, complex code or math, and tasks with many constraints.
- Skip it, or turn the depth down, for simple lookups, formatting, rewriting, quick classification and chatty drafting. It adds delay and cost without much benefit.
- Prompting differs: with a reasoning model you generally state the goal, constraints and how to judge success rather than dictating each thought. The "think step by step" phrase from older prompting advice is often unnecessary or redundant (domain 2.4).

> **Watch out:** Reasoning is not a guarantee of correctness. A reasoning model can still hallucinate, and its visible or summarized reasoning is not a verified proof. Treat the final answer the way you treat any output: check it.

## Try it yourself

1. Take a plausible but tricky networking puzzle with placeholder data, such as: `Hosts 192.0.2.10/26 and 192.0.2.70/26 are on the same switch with no router. Can they communicate, and why or why not? Show your work.` Run it first in a fast or default mode and then in the app's thinking or reasoning mode, in two different assistants. Compare accuracy, length and wait time.
2. Ask each assistant to classify 20 invented firewall log lines as allow, deny or unknown. Run once on a fast model and once on a reasoning model. Is the extra thinking worth it for this task?
3. Open each vendor's current models page (Anthropic's models overview, OpenAI's models page, Google's Gemini API models page) and write down the tier names and the date you checked. Compare with this lesson to see what has changed.

## Key takeaways

- ChatGPT (OpenAI GPT), Gemini (Google) and Claude (Anthropic) are products built on separate model families.
- Each family offers tiers: flagship, balanced and fast/lite, plus specialized models for image, voice and security work.
- As of October 2026: Claude has Fable 5.1, Opus 5.5, Sonnet 5.5 and Haiku 5.5; OpenAI has GPT-6 Astra, GPT-6.1 Sol, GPT-6.1 Terra and GPT-6 Luna; Google has Gemini 4 Argon and Gemini 3.8 Flash, with Flash-Lite models below.
- Names are vendor-specific and short-lived; learn the tier pattern and check the vendor page.
- Reasoning ("thinking") models spend hidden tokens planning before answering, controlled by an effort or level setting.
- Reasoning tokens are billed as output and share the context window.
- Use reasoning for hard multi-step problems and fast models for simple, high-volume work; always verify the output.
