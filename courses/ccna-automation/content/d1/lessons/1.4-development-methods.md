## Why methods matter to a network engineer

Automation work is software work. Whether you are building a provisioning tool with three colleagues or contributing to a large NetDevOps platform, the team needs an agreed way to decide what to build, in what order, and how to deliver it. The exam asks you to compare three families: **waterfall**, **agile** and **lean**. Expect scenario questions: a description of how a team works, and you name the method (or spot the one that does not fit).

## Waterfall: sequential phases

**Waterfall** runs a project as a sequence of phases, each finished and signed off before the next starts, like water flowing down steps:

```text
Requirements -> Design -> Implementation -> Testing (verification) -> Deployment -> Maintenance
```

Names and the exact number of phases vary by source; the defining features do not:

- **All requirements are gathered and fixed up front**, then documented in detail.
- Each phase produces documents or artefacts that the next phase consumes; there are **phase gates** (reviews and sign-offs).
- **Testing happens late**, after implementation is complete.
- The customer sees **working software near the end**.
- Change is expensive: a requirement discovered during testing means going back up the waterfall.

Waterfall works when requirements are genuinely stable and well understood, when the cost of change is very high (hardware, safety-critical or regulated systems), or when a contract demands fixed scope and documentation. It struggles when requirements are uncertain, because mistakes surface late, when they cost the most to fix.

> **Exam tip:** Keywords that point to waterfall: "fixed requirements", "complete specification before development", "sign-off", "each phase must complete before the next", "testing after development is complete", "one big release".

## Agile: short iterations and working software

**Agile** is a set of values and principles, not one process. It was written down in the **Manifesto for Agile Software Development** (2001), which values:

| We value... | ...over |
|---|---|
| **Individuals and interactions** | processes and tools |
| **Working software** | comprehensive documentation |
| **Customer collaboration** | contract negotiation |
| **Responding to change** | following a plan |

The manifesto adds that there is value in the items on the right, but the items on the left are valued more. Its twelve principles include delivering working software frequently ("from a couple of weeks to a couple of months, with a preference to the shorter timescale"), welcoming changing requirements even late in development, business people and developers working together daily, working software as the primary measure of progress, sustainable pace, and regular reflection ("at regular intervals, the team reflects on how to become more effective").

In practice agile teams:

- Keep a prioritised **backlog** of small items (user stories) instead of a fixed specification.
- Work in short **iterations** (in **Scrum**, *sprints*: fixed-length events of one month or less, often two weeks), each producing a usable increment that stakeholders review.
- Plan, build, test and integrate **continuously** inside each iteration, so testing is not a final phase; TDD and CI (lessons 1.3 and domain 4) are common agile practices.
- Hold a short daily meeting (the Daily Scrum or stand-up), a review (demo) and a retrospective each iteration.

Scrum names three accountabilities: the **product owner** (owns and orders the backlog), the **Scrum master** (coaches the process and removes impediments) and the **developers**. **Kanban** is another common agile and lean approach: work flows across a board (To do, In progress, Done) with **work-in-progress (WIP) limits** rather than fixed-length sprints.

> **Watch out:** Agile does not mean "no documentation" or "no planning". The manifesto values working software *more than* comprehensive documentation; agile teams still plan, but they plan continuously and in small pieces.

## Lean: eliminate waste, optimise flow

**Lean** comes from manufacturing, specifically the Toyota Production System, and was adapted to software by Mary and Tom Poppendieck in *Lean Software Development: An Agile Toolkit* (2003). Its central idea: **everything that does not add value for the customer is waste**, and the goal is to deliver value with as little waste and delay as possible. The Poppendiecks summarise it in seven principles:

| Principle | Meaning in a software team |
|---|---|
| **Eliminate waste** | Remove anything that does not add customer value |
| **Amplify learning** | Short feedback loops, experiments, iterations |
| **Decide as late as possible** | Keep options open until you have the facts; avoid irreversible early decisions |
| **Deliver as fast as possible** | Small batches and short cycle time, so feedback arrives quickly |
| **Empower the team** | The people doing the work make the decisions about how |
| **Build integrity in** | Quality built in from the start (automated tests, refactoring), not inspected in at the end |
| **See (optimise) the whole** | Optimise the entire value stream, not one team or step in isolation |

The Poppendiecks map manufacturing's seven wastes to software: **partially done work** (code written but not deployed), **extra processes**, **extra features** nobody asked for, **task switching**, **waiting** (for approvals, environments, reviews), **motion** (hand-offs between people or teams) and **defects**. Lean teams find waste with **value stream mapping**: drawing every step from request to delivery and measuring how much of the elapsed time actually adds value. They limit work in progress, pull work when there is capacity rather than pushing it, and aim for a **minimum viable product (MVP)** to learn early what customers really need.

Lean and agile overlap heavily (Kanban, short cycles and continuous improvement belong to both), so the exam distinguishes them by emphasis: agile talks about **iterations, collaboration and responding to change**; lean talks about **waste, flow, value stream and WIP limits**.

## Side by side

| | Waterfall | Agile | Lean |
|---|---|---|---|
| Core idea | Plan everything, then execute phases in order | Deliver working software in short iterations, adapt to change | Maximise customer value by eliminating waste and improving flow |
| Requirements | Fixed and complete up front | Evolving backlog, reprioritised every iteration | Discovered through fast feedback; decide as late as possible |
| Delivery | One release at the end | An increment every iteration | Small batches, continuous flow |
| Testing | A phase after implementation | Continuous within each iteration | Built in from the start ("build integrity in") |
| Customer involvement | Mostly at the start (requirements) and end (acceptance) | Continuous collaboration and review | Value defined by the customer; feedback drives decisions |
| Handles change | Poorly and expensively | Expected and welcomed | Expected; options kept open |
| Typical artefacts | Requirements specification, design documents, sign-offs | Backlog, sprint board, increment, retrospective | Value stream map, Kanban board with WIP limits |
| Good fit | Stable, well-understood, contract- or regulation-driven work | Uncertain or changing requirements, need for early feedback | Improving the speed and efficiency of an existing delivery process |

> **Real world:** Many network teams combine these. A data-centre refresh with procurement lead times and change-board approvals often runs as waterfall, while the automation code that configures it is built in two-week sprints, and the operations team uses a Kanban board with WIP limits for incoming change requests.

## Lab it yourself

No equipment needed; a browser and a text editor are enough on any computer.

1. Read the four values and twelve principles of the Agile Manifesto at agilemanifesto.org and mark which ones a waterfall project would struggle with.
2. Take a recent change you made (say, a VLAN rollout to 40 switches). Write it as a waterfall plan (phases with sign-offs) and as a backlog of agile user stories ("As a NOC engineer I want...").
3. Draw a value stream map of how a change request moves through your team today: every step, wait and hand-off, with rough times. Circle the lean wastes.
4. Create a three-column Kanban board (a GitHub Projects board, a spreadsheet or sticky notes) with a WIP limit of 2 on "In progress", and use it for a week.

## Key takeaways

- **Waterfall**: sequential phases with sign-offs, all requirements up front, testing late, one delivery at the end; good for stable, well-defined work, costly when requirements change.
- **Agile**: the Agile Manifesto's four values (individuals and interactions, working software, customer collaboration, responding to change); short iterations such as Scrum sprints, a prioritised backlog, continuous testing and customer feedback.
- **Lean**: from the Toyota Production System via the Poppendiecks; eliminate waste, amplify learning, decide late, deliver fast, empower the team, build integrity in, see the whole.
- Software wastes: partially done work, extra processes, extra features, task switching, waiting, hand-offs, defects.
- Agile emphasises iterations and responding to change; lean emphasises waste, flow, value streams and WIP limits; Kanban sits in both.
