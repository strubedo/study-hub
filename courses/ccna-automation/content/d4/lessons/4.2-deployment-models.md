## Where the application runs

A **deployment model** answers "whose infrastructure does the application run on, and who else shares it?" The definitions most courses and vendors use come from **NIST SP 800-145**, *The NIST Definition of Cloud Computing*. It names four models: private, community, public and hybrid. The exam lists private, public, hybrid and **edge**; edge is not a NIST model but a location (lesson 4.1), and in practice it is combined with one of the others.

NIST also lists five essential characteristics that make something a *cloud* rather than just a data centre: **on-demand self-service**, **broad network access**, **resource pooling** (multi-tenant), **rapid elasticity** and **measured service** (you are metered for what you use). A rack of servers that needs a ticket and two weeks to get a VM is not a cloud, whoever owns it.

## The models

**Private cloud.** NIST: infrastructure "provisioned for exclusive use by a single organization comprising multiple consumers (e.g., business units)". It "may be owned, managed, and operated by the organization, a third party, or some combination of them, and it may exist on or off premises." The defining attribute is **exclusive use by one organisation**, not location: a dedicated hosted environment in a provider's building is still private.

**Public cloud.** Provisioned "for open use by the general public" and it "exists on the premises of the cloud provider". You rent capacity on shared infrastructure (AWS, Microsoft Azure, Google Cloud and others), pay for what you use, and can scale in minutes.

**Hybrid cloud.** "A composition of two or more distinct cloud infrastructures (private, community, or public) that remain unique entities, but are bound together by standardized or proprietary technology that enables data and application portability (e.g., cloud bursting for load balancing between clouds)." The parts stay separate; what makes it hybrid is the connection and portability between them.

**Community cloud** (NIST only, rarely examined): shared by several organisations with common concerns, such as government agencies with the same compliance rules.

**Edge.** Compute at or near the site where data is produced: a branch server, an industrial gateway, or an application hosted on a router with Cisco IOx. Edge sites are usually managed from, and send results to, a private or public cloud.

## Comparing the models

| Attribute | Private | Public | Hybrid | Edge |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Who uses the infrastructure | One organisation | Many tenants | Both, connected | One organisation, per site |
| Where it is | On or off premises | Provider's premises | Both | At or near the data source |
| Up-front cost | High (hardware, facilities) unless hosted | None: pay per use (operating expense) | Mixed | Hardware at every site |
| Elasticity | Limited by what you bought | Very high, near instant | Burst from private to public | Low: small, fixed devices |
| Control and customisation | Highest | Lowest (provider controls hardware and hypervisor) | Choose per workload | High, but spread over many sites |
| Compliance and data location | Easiest to prove | Choose regions; shared responsibility | Keep regulated data private | Data can stay on site |
| Operations burden | You run everything | Provider runs the infrastructure | Two environments to integrate | Many remote sites to manage |
| Latency to on-site devices | Depends on distance | Depends on region distance | Mixed | Lowest |

> **Exam tip:** Match the clue to the model. "Exclusive use by one organisation" or "full control of hardware" is private. "Pay as you go", "scale in minutes" or "no hardware to buy" is public. "Keep the database on premises but burst the web tier into the cloud" or "two environments linked for portability" is hybrid. "Process data where it is produced" or "keep working when the WAN is down" is edge.

## Responsibility moves with the model

In a public cloud the provider secures the physical data centre, hardware and virtualisation layer, and **you** secure what you put on it: the operating systems you deploy (for IaaS), your applications, identities, data and network rules. This **shared responsibility model** is why a misconfigured public storage bucket is the customer's fault, not the provider's. In a private cloud you own every layer. A hybrid design has to apply consistent identity, firewall and logging policy across both sides, which is where most of the complexity sits.

> **Watch out:** "Private" does not mean "on premises" and "public" does not mean "insecure". A private cloud can be hosted by a third party; a public cloud tenant can be isolated by its own virtual networks, encryption and access policy. The models differ in who shares the infrastructure and who operates it.

## Choosing a model

Typical drivers:

- **Unpredictable or seasonal load**, a new product, or no capital budget: public.
- **Strict data-residency or regulatory rules**, steady predictable load, or specialised hardware: private.
- **A mix**: hybrid, for example a public-cloud web front end with an on-premises database, or disaster recovery in the cloud for an on-premises system.
- **Real-time local processing or poor connectivity**: edge, with results flowing to a private or public cloud.

## Lab it yourself

Any computer with a browser:

1. Open the free-tier or pricing calculator of any public cloud provider and price one small Linux VM running all month. Then price the same VM for eight hours a day. The difference is "measured service" in action.
2. Read the shared responsibility page of the same provider and list three things the provider secures and three that remain yours.
3. For your own network (or a lab one), write down one workload that suits each model: private, public, hybrid and edge, with the one attribute that decided it.

## Key takeaways

- NIST SP 800-145: private = exclusive use by one organisation (on or off premises); public = open use, on the provider's premises; hybrid = two or more distinct clouds bound together for data and application portability.
- Cloud means on-demand self-service, broad network access, resource pooling, rapid elasticity and measured service.
- Public trades control for elasticity and no up-front cost; private trades cost for control and compliance; hybrid combines them at the price of integration work.
- Edge runs compute at the data source for latency, bandwidth, locality and resilience, usually alongside a private or public cloud.
- In the public cloud, responsibility is shared: the provider secures the infrastructure, you secure your workloads, identities and data.
