## One question, three platforms

Most connectivity problems start with the same four questions about the local host: what address does it have, where does it send traffic, who is it talking to on the local link, and what sockets are open. Every platform has a tool for each.

| Question | Linux (iproute2) | Windows | Cisco IOS |
|---|---|---|---|
| Interface addresses and state | `ip addr`, `ip -br addr`, `ip link` | `ipconfig`, `ipconfig /all` | `show ip interface brief` |
| Routing table | `ip route`, `ip route get <dst>` | `route print`, `netstat -rn` | `show ip route` |
| ARP / neighbour cache | `ip neigh` | `arp -a` | `show ip arp` |
| Listening ports and connections | `ss` | `netstat -ano` | `show tcp brief`, `show control-plane host open-ports` |
| DHCP release/renew | `dhclient -r` / `dhclient`, or NetworkManager | `ipconfig /release`, `/renew` | `release dhcp`/`renew dhcp` on an interface set to `ip address dhcp` (exec command) |
| DNS cache flush | `resolvectl flush-caches` | `ipconfig /flushdns` | `clear host *` |

Legacy tools still show up in older documents: `ifconfig`, `route`, `arp` and `netstat` come from the **net-tools** package, which is deprecated on Linux and often not installed by default. The iproute2 commands above replace them and show more (multiple addresses, policy routing, namespaces).

## Interface addressing

```text
$ ip -br addr
lo               UNKNOWN        127.0.0.1/8
eth0             UP             192.0.2.10/24 fe80::21a:2bff:fe3c:4d5e/64

$ ip addr show eth0
2: eth0: <BROADCAST,MULTICAST,UP,LOWER_UP> mtu 1500 qdisc fq_codel state UP group default qlen 1000
    link/ether 00:1a:2b:3c:4d:5e brd ff:ff:ff:ff:ff:ff
    inet 192.0.2.10/24 brd 192.0.2.255 scope global dynamic eth0
       valid_lft 85412sec preferred_lft 85412sec
```

Check the flags first. `UP` is the administrative state; `LOWER_UP` means a link is detected (cable and far end up). `UP` without `LOWER_UP`, or `NO-CARRIER`, is a layer 1 or layer 2 problem. `dynamic` and `valid_lft` mean the address came from DHCP and show the remaining lease. `ip -s link` adds RX/TX packet, error and drop counters; rising errors point at duplex, cabling or MTU problems.

Windows:

```text
C:\> ipconfig /all

Ethernet adapter Ethernet:
   Physical Address. . . . . . . . . : 00-1A-2B-3C-4D-5E
   DHCP Enabled. . . . . . . . . . . : Yes
   IPv4 Address. . . . . . . . . . . : 192.0.2.10(Preferred)
   Subnet Mask . . . . . . . . . . . : 255.255.255.0
   Lease Obtained. . . . . . . . . . : Friday, October 30, 2026 8:01:14 AM
   Lease Expires . . . . . . . . . . : Saturday, October 31, 2026 8:01:14 AM
   Default Gateway . . . . . . . . . : 192.0.2.1
   DHCP Server . . . . . . . . . . . : 192.0.2.1
   DNS Servers . . . . . . . . . . . : 192.0.2.53
```

An address of **169.254.x.x** with the label `Autoconfiguration IPv4 Address` means the DHCP exchange failed and Windows self-assigned a link-local address. No gateway is shown, so only the local link works. Look at the DHCP server, relay or VLAN, not at routing.

## Routing table

`ip route` and `route print` were covered in the routing lessons. Two diagnostics matter most: `ip route get <destination>` (Linux), which tells you the exact route, interface and source address the kernel would use, and `route print` on Windows, where you read the metric column when two default routes exist. The Cisco equivalent for one destination is `show ip route 203.0.113.9`, which prints the matching entry or `% Network not in table`.

## Neighbour and ARP cache

```text
$ ip neigh
192.0.2.1 dev eth0 lladdr 00:50:56:aa:bb:cc REACHABLE
192.0.2.20 dev eth0 lladdr 00:50:56:11:22:33 STALE
192.0.2.99 dev eth0  FAILED

C:\> arp -a
Interface: 192.0.2.10 --- 0x4
  Internet Address      Physical Address      Type
  192.0.2.1             00-50-56-aa-bb-cc     dynamic
  192.0.2.255           ff-ff-ff-ff-ff-ff     static
  224.0.0.22            01-00-5e-00-00-16     static
```

The cache proves whether layer 2 resolution worked. If the default gateway is `FAILED` or `INCOMPLETE` (Linux) or missing (Windows), ARP requests are going unanswered: wrong VLAN, wrong address, a down switch port or a filtered gateway. If it holds an unexpected MAC, suspect a duplicate IP or ARP spoofing. `STALE` is normal, not an error. Clear entries with `ip neigh flush dev eth0`, `arp -d *` (administrator) or `clear ip arp` on IOS. See the ARP lesson for state details.

## Sockets: ss and netstat

`ss` (socket statistics) reads kernel socket data directly and replaces `netstat` on Linux. Common flags: `-t` TCP, `-u` UDP, `-l` listening, `-a` all, `-n` numeric, `-p` process (needs privileges), `-s` summary, `-o` timers.

```text
$ ss -tulnp
Netid State  Recv-Q Send-Q Local Address:Port  Peer Address:Port Process
udp   UNCONN 0      0      127.0.0.53%lo:53    0.0.0.0:*         users:(("systemd-resolve",pid=612,fd=13))
tcp   LISTEN 0      4096   0.0.0.0:22          0.0.0.0:*         users:(("sshd",pid=801,fd=3))
tcp   LISTEN 0      511    127.0.0.1:8080      0.0.0.0:*         users:(("nginx",pid=915,fd=6))

$ ss -tn state established '( dport = :443 )'
Recv-Q Send-Q Local Address:Port    Peer Address:Port
0      0      192.0.2.10:50718      198.51.100.80:443
```

Interpreting this: 22 listens on all addresses (`0.0.0.0`), so remote hosts can connect; 8080 listens on `127.0.0.1` only, so it is unreachable from another host even if the firewall is open (a very common cause of "connection refused" from outside). For a listening TCP socket, `Send-Q` is the configured backlog and `Recv-Q` the number of connections waiting to be accepted; for a connected socket they are unsent and unread bytes. `ss -s` and `ss state time-wait` show state counts (see the TCP state lesson).

Windows has `netstat -ano` (all connections, numeric, with owning PID) and `netstat -b` (executable name, administrator only). Map the PID with `tasklist /fi "pid eq 5120"`. PowerShell offers `Get-NetTCPConnection -State Listen` and `Get-NetUDPEndpoint`.

```text
C:\> netstat -ano | findstr :443
  TCP    192.0.2.10:50718       198.51.100.80:443      ESTABLISHED     5120
  TCP    0.0.0.0:443            0.0.0.0:0              LISTENING       4
```

On IOS, `show tcp brief` lists the router's own TCP sockets, and `show ip sockets` shows UDP.

## Testing a port

Distinguishing the failure types by what you see:

| Symptom | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Immediate "connection refused" (TCP RST) | Host reachable; nothing is listening, or a firewall rejects with a reset |
| Timeout, no reply | Packets dropped: firewall drop, wrong route, host down |
| ICMP unreachable (type 3) | A router or host reported the reason (net, host, port, admin prohibited) |
| Connects, then application error | Network path is fine; look at the application |

`nc -vz 198.51.100.80 443` (Linux), `Test-NetConnection 198.51.100.80 -Port 443` (PowerShell), and `telnet 198.51.100.80 443` are quick port checks.

## Lab it yourself

1. Run `ip -br addr`, `ip -s link show eth0` and `ip route get 8.8.8.8`; note the source address and the interface.
2. Unplug the cable (or `ip link set eth0 down`) and compare `ip link` flags with `NO-CARRIER`.
3. Run `ss -tulnp`, start `python3 -m http.server 8000 --bind 127.0.0.1`, find it, and then confirm that `curl` from another machine to port 8000 is refused. Restart it with `--bind 0.0.0.0` and confirm the `ss` line changes.
4. On Windows run `ipconfig /all`, `route print -4`, `arp -a` and `netstat -ano`, and explain where each answer fits in the table above.
5. On IOS compare `show ip interface brief`, `show ip arp` and `show ip route`, then `ping` a neighbour and watch the ARP entry appear.

## Key takeaways

- Each question (address, route, neighbour, sockets) has a Linux, a Windows and a Cisco command; iproute2 and `ss` supersede the deprecated net-tools `ifconfig`, `route`, `arp` and `netstat`.
- `UP` is administrative; `LOWER_UP` or carrier shows the physical state.
- A 169.254.x.x address means DHCP failed; look at the DHCP path, not routing.
- `ip route get` and `route print` show which route is actually chosen.
- A `FAILED`/`INCOMPLETE` gateway entry in the neighbour table means ARP is not being answered.
- `ss -tulnp` (Linux) and `netstat -ano` (Windows) show listeners, the bind address and the owning process.
- A service bound to 127.0.0.1 is not reachable remotely.
- Refused means a reset came back; a timeout means silence.
